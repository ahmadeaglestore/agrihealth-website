White Mold (Sclerotinia Rot)

Soft, wet stem rot covered with white cotton-like fungus and hard black seed-like bodies; plants wilt.

Overview

White mold, also called Sclerotinia rot or stem rot, is caused by a soil fungus that can attack hundreds of plant species, including brinjal, mustard and canola, cabbage and cauliflower, chickpea, potato, tomato and beans. Its clearest sign is thick white cotton-like growth on a soft, rotting stem, with hard black bodies called sclerotia, like mouse droppings, on or inside the stem.

It is a disease of cool, moist weather, so in Pakistan it is mostly seen in the winter (rabi) season around flowering, after rain, fog, heavy dew or heavy irrigation, and in thick crops where the leaves stay wet. It has been recorded in Pakistan on safflower at Islamabad. In mustard and canola the stem is girdled, so the plant ripens early, falls over and loses seed.

Symptoms

  • Pale, water-soaked patches on stems, branches, leaves or fruit, often starting where old flowers have fallen
  • Thick white cotton-like fungus grows over the wet, rotting patch
  • Hard black bodies (sclerotia), like mouse droppings, form in the white growth and inside hollow stems
  • Stems turn bleached or straw-coloured, then dry and shred
  • Branches or whole plants above the rotten part wilt and die; mustard and canola plants ripen early and fall over
  • Cabbage heads and brinjal fruit can turn into a soft, wet rot with white growth

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (soil-borne fungus that forms hard black sclerotia).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Sclerotia stay alive in the soil for a long time and start new infections when the soil is wet
  • In cool, wet weather the sclerotia make tiny mushroom-like cups that shoot spores into the air; wind carries them to the crop
  • Spores first grow on old or fallen flowers, then move into stems, leaves and fruit
  • Cool, moist weather, fog, heavy dew and frequent or heavy irrigation
  • Thick crops, low-lying fields with poor air movement, too much nitrogen and broad-leaved weeds
  • Uncleaned seed with sclerotia mixed in

What to Do

  • Pull out plants with white growth and black sclerotia, put them in a bag and take them out of the field; burn them or bury them deep
  • Do not leave rotten stems or sclerotia lying on the soil, as they will infect the next crop
  • Reduce irrigation, water in the morning and do not let water stand in the field
  • Remove broad-leaved weeds and thin out very thick patches so the crop dries faster
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide spray is advised; sprays can only protect healthy plants and cannot save rotten stems

Don't

  • Do not move soil, straw or crop remains from an infected field to clean fields

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many plants in a field show white growth and black sclerotia, especially in mustard, canola or chickpea at flowering.

Prevention

  • In fields that had white mold, grow cereals such as wheat, maize or rice for at least three to four years before a susceptible crop
  • Avoid sowing mustard, canola or vegetables next to fields that had serious white mold in recent years
  • Plough the field soon after harvest to bury crop remains
  • Sow clean seed that has no sclerotia mixed in
  • Do not sow too thick; give wider spacing and run rows along the wind direction so the crop dries quickly
  • Use balanced fertiliser, avoid too much nitrogen, and control broad-leaved weeds
  • Avoid wounding plants and cabbage heads during field work

Problems That Look Similar

  • Southern blight (collar rot)

    White fungus at the stem base at soil level with many small, round, brown bodies like mustard seeds, mostly in hot weather, not large black sclerotia.

  • Fusarium or Verticillium wilt

    Leaves yellow and wilt, often on one side, with brown streaks inside the stem, but no white cotton-like growth outside.

  • Phytophthora blight

    Dark brown or black water-soaked rot at the stem base after heavy irrigation, without white cottony growth and black sclerotia.
