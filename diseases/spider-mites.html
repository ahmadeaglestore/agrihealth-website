Spider Mites (Red Mites)

Fine pale speckles on leaves that turn bronze, with tiny moving mites and fine webs underneath.

Overview

Spider mites are not insects but tiny eight-legged relatives of spiders, about the size of a full stop. They live mostly on the underside of leaves and suck out the contents of leaf cells. The two-spotted mite is pale yellow-green with two dark spots, while other kinds are red, which is why they are often called red mites. They attack tomato, brinjal, okra, cucurbits, cotton, chilli and many other plants; citrus has its own similar mites.

Mites breed fastest in hot, dry, dusty weather: in hot summers one generation can take only about 10–14 days, so numbers can explode in a short time. In Pakistan, hot and dry spells are the main danger period. Repeated sprays of broad-spectrum insecticides for other pests often make mite outbreaks worse, because they kill the predators that eat mites.

Symptoms

  • Tiny pale yellow or white dots (stippling) on the upper side of leaves
  • Leaves turn pale, then bronze, reddish or rusty brown, and look dusty
  • Fine silky webs under leaves and between leaves and stems when numbers are high
  • Tiny moving dots under the leaf; tap a leaf over white paper and watch them crawl
  • Leaves dry and drop early; plants weaken and set fewer flowers and fruit
  • On citrus: pale or silvery speckling on leaves and green fruit; heavy attack dries leaves and causes leaf drop

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Two-spotted spider mite Tetranychus urticae and related Tetranychus mites; on citrus, related species such as the citrus red mite Panonychus citri.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Hot, dry weather speeds up breeding, so numbers rise very quickly
  • Plants short of water and dusty leaves (near roads and dirt tracks) suffer more
  • Broad-spectrum insecticides kill predatory mites, ladybirds and lacewings that keep spider mites down, and some even increase mite breeding
  • Mites survive on weeds and crop remains and move onto crops when the weather warms up

What to Do

  • Check the underside of leaves with a hand lens, or tap leaves over white paper, especially in hot dry spells
  • If only a few leaves or plants are hit, pick off and destroy those leaves or plants
  • Water the crop on time so it is not short of water, and reduce dust, for example by wetting dusty paths
  • On small plots, a strong spray of plain water on the underside of leaves knocks many mites off
  • Give predatory mites, ladybirds and lacewings a chance by not spraying broad-spectrum insecticides
  • If mites keep spreading, ask your agriculture officer whether a product that works on mites is needed and which one is approved

Don't

  • Don't spray broad-spectrum insecticides again and again; this usually makes mites worse
  • Don't spray oils or soaps in hot midday sun; they can burn the leaves

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if leaves are bronzing and webbed on many plants, or if mites are still spreading after you have taken the steps above.

Prevention

  • Keep fields and field edges free of weeds that carry mites
  • Irrigate regularly in hot weather so plants are not stressed
  • Use pesticides only when really needed, to protect natural enemies
  • Remove and destroy crop remains after harvest
  • Check crops every week from the start of hot, dry weather

Problems That Look Similar

  • Thrips damage

    Silvery scraped streaks and upward-curled leaves, with slender insects in buds and young leaves; no webs.

  • Jassid (leafhopper) damage

    Leaf edges turn yellow then red and curl down; small green wedge-shaped insects walk sideways or jump; no webs.

  • Whitefly

    Tiny white flying insects and sticky honeydew under the leaves; no fine webs.
