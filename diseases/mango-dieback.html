Dieback of Mango

Twigs and branches dry from the tip downwards, leaves look scorched, and gum oozes from cracks.

Overview

Dieback is a fungal disease of mango in which twigs and branches dry from the tip backwards. Leaves on the affected shoots turn brown and look as if burnt by fire. When an affected twig is split lengthwise, the wood inside shows brown streaks. Cracks with gum appear on branches. It is seen most on older trees, but grafted nursery plants can also die if the graft joint is infected.

The fungus lives on dead twigs and plant debris and enters mostly through wounds, such as pruning cuts, borer holes and cracks. Trees under stress from drought, heat, poor nutrition or poor care are hit hardest. In Sindh, researchers linked Lasiodiplodia theobromae to mango decline with gummosis and found the disease worse in orchards short of water. Indian IPM guides note it is most visible in October–November. Dieback starts at the shoot tips and moves slowly down; this is different from sudden death (quick decline), which starts at the trunk and can kill a whole tree within months.

Symptoms

  • Young twigs lose colour and dry from the tip backwards, first on a few branches
  • Leaves on dying twigs turn brown, their edges roll upwards and they look scorched by fire; dry leaves may stay on the twig
  • Brown streaks inside the wood when an affected twig is split lengthwise
  • Cracks in the bark of twigs and branches with yellow to brown gum oozing out
  • In bad cases bark splits on larger branches and the trunk, and big branches dry
  • Grafted nursery plants dry and die when the graft joint is infected

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Lasiodiplodia theobromae (fungus; older name Botryodiplodia theobromae).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Dead infected twigs left on the tree carry the fungus to the next season
  • Infected grafting wood (bud sticks) and nursery plants spread it to new orchards
  • Pruning cuts that are not covered, borer holes and other wounds let the fungus in
  • Uncleaned pruning tools can move it from tree to tree
  • Stress from water shortage, high summer heat, poor nutrition and late control of stem borers makes trees more likely to get sick

What to Do

  • Cut dried twigs and branches well below the dried part, into healthy green wood (about 10 cm below the last brown streak)
  • Collect all cut branches and burn them away from the orchard
  • Cover large cuts with a protective paste or wound sealant recommended by your agriculture department
  • Clean pruning tools between cuts and between trees
  • Check that trees are getting enough water and manure, and fix any water shortage
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide is needed after pruning, and which product is approved

Don't

  • Do not leave dead branches on the tree or prunings lying in the orchard
  • Do not take grafting wood from trees with dieback

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the Mango Research Institute if many branches are drying, if gum oozes from the trunk, or if the tree wilts quickly, because sudden death (quick decline) needs urgent checking.

Prevention

  • Use grafting wood and nursery plants only from healthy, disease-free trees
  • Prune out dead and diseased wood every year after harvest and burn it
  • Seal pruning cuts and avoid bark injuries from tools and machinery
  • Control stem and bark borers on time as advised by your agriculture officer
  • Give regular, sufficient irrigation and balanced manure to avoid stress
  • Inspect trees regularly, especially after the rains, and act on the first dry twigs

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango sudden death (quick decline)

    Starts at the trunk or main branches with gum, bark-beetle holes and dark streaks, and the whole tree can wilt in a short time; dieback starts at twig tips and moves down slowly.

  • Mango anthracnose

    Twig tips may dry, but young leaves, flowers and fruit also show black spots, mostly after rain.

  • Stem or branch borer damage

    Holes in the bark with sawdust-like powder or frass coming out; the branch above the hole dries.
