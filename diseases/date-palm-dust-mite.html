Dust Mite (Ghubar) of Date Palm

Fine grey webs full of dust cover green date bunches; fruits turn brown, scarred, cracked and shrivelled.

Overview

The date dust mite is a tiny spider mite, hard to see without a hand lens. It feeds on young green dates and covers them and the fruit strands with fine, cream to grey silky webs. Dust and sand stick to the webs, so the bunch looks as if it is covered in dust, which is why it is called 'ghubar' (dust). Feeding scars the fruit skin: fruits turn brown and scabby, then harden, crack and shrivel, and badly attacked fruit may drop. Such fruit has little or no market value. The mites gather mostly near the cap of the fruit, where they also lay eggs.

Hot, dry summer weather favours it. Numbers rise fast while the fruits are green and full of water, and fall once the fruits turn yellow or red. One generation takes only about 10 to 15 days, so there are many generations in a season. After the fruit season the mites move to the fronds, frond bases and fibres, and to grasses and crops under the palms, such as khabbal grass, sorghum, maize and sugarcane. It is a serious pest in North Africa and the Middle East. A scientific database lists a record from Pakistan, but how common it is here is not well known, and other spider mites can make similar webs, so get it confirmed by your agriculture officer.

Symptoms

  • Fine, cream to grey silky webs over the fruit strands and young green dates, with dust and sand stuck in them
  • Bunches look dusty and dull
  • Tiny moving specks (mites) and eggs under the webs, mostly near the fruit cap; a hand lens helps
  • Fruit skin scarred, turning brown or reddish-brown and scabby
  • Fruits harden, crack and shrivel, and cannot be sold
  • In heavy attack, green fruits drop

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Oligonychus afrasiaticus (Old World date mite, also called 'bou faroua' or 'ghubar'), a spider mite.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Hot, dry summer weather lets numbers rise fast
  • Green fruits are attacked most; yellow or red fruits resist
  • Mites spread through the air, so sticky bands and nets on bunch stalks did not stop them in field trials
  • Grasses and crops under the palms, such as khabbal grass, sorghum, maize and sugarcane, can host the mite
  • Mites survive the off-season on fronds, in frond bases and fibres, and on young fronds
  • The dust caught in the webs shelters the mites

What to Do

  • From fruit set, check green bunches every week in hot weather for webbing; use a hand lens to see the mites
  • Mark infested palms and check the palms around them
  • Keep grasses and weeds under the palms under control
  • Protect natural enemies, such as predatory mites and small ladybird beetles, by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum sprays
  • If webbing is spreading on green bunches, ask your agriculture officer which miticide is recommended and when; treatment works best early, while fruits are still green

Don't

  • Don't use ordinary insecticides that do not kill mites; they kill natural enemies and mites can increase

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the Date Palm Research Institute as soon as dusty webbing appears on green bunches, so the mite can be identified and treated early; fruit that is already scarred cannot be repaired.

Prevention

  • Keep the ground under palms clean of grasses such as khabbal, and avoid growing sorghum, maize or sugarcane right under the palms where possible
  • After harvest, prune old dry fronds and clean frond bases where mites hide
  • Check young fronds before fruit set for mites
  • Work with neighbours, as mites move between orchards through the air

Problems That Look Similar

  • Spider mites (red mites)

    Related mites that make similar fine webs on vegetables, cotton and other crops; see that guide.

  • Lesser date moth

    Young dates have a small hole near the stalk with a caterpillar inside, and dry fruits hang tied by a few silk threads, not covered by dusty webs.

  • Ordinary dust

    Dust from wind or roads brushes off easily, with no webs and no scarred fruit skin.
