Virus Diseases of Vegetables (Mosaic, Yellowing, Curling)

Leaves with light and dark green mosaic, yellow veins, curling or crinkling; plants stunted with poor fruit.

Overview

Viruses are tiny germs that live inside plant cells. You cannot see them, and they do not make powder, rot or holes. Instead they change the colour and shape of the leaves: light and dark green mosaic, yellow veins or patches, ring spots, curled, crinkled or narrow leaves, and short, bushy plants. Fruit can be small, mottled or misshapen. Once a plant is infected it stays infected for life, and no spray can cure it.

In Pakistan, virus diseases cause big losses in tomato, chilli, okra, melons and gourds, especially in the warm months when whiteflies, aphids and thrips are many. Most vegetable viruses are carried from plant to plant by these insects; some come with seed, seedlings or planting material such as potato tubers and onion sets, and some spread on hands and tools. In onion, onion yellow dwarf virus causes yellow streaks and flattened, falling leaves. On brinjal, mosaic is caused by viruses, while 'little leaf' (tiny leaves, bushy plants and leafy flowers) is caused by a phytoplasma spread by leafhoppers and is managed in the same way. Many single viruses have their own guides on this site.

Symptoms

  • Mosaic: patches of light green or yellow mixed with dark green on the leaves
  • Yellow veins, or yellow streaks and patches on the leaves (for example yellow streaks at the base of onion leaves)
  • Leaves curl up or down, crinkle, become thick and leathery, or stay small and narrow
  • Ring spots or dead brown streaks on leaves, stems or fruit
  • Plants stay short and bushy, with short gaps between the leaves
  • Few flowers and fruit; fruit is small, mottled, bumpy or misshapen
  • At first usually single plants here and there, then spreading along the rows over the weeks

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Many plant viruses, e.g. whitefly-borne leaf curl viruses, aphid-borne mosaic viruses, thrips-borne tospoviruses and seed- or touch-borne tobamoviruses.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Whiteflies spread the leaf curl and yellow vein viruses of tomato, chilli, okra and melons
  • Aphids spread mosaic viruses of melons, gourds, chilli and potato, and onion yellow dwarf virus; they can pass some viruses in a very short feed, so sprays cannot fully stop them
  • Thrips spread tospoviruses such as tomato spotted wilt virus and iris yellow spot virus of onion
  • Infected seed, seedlings, potato tubers and onion sets carry viruses into new fields
  • Some viruses, like tomato mosaic virus, spread on hands, tools and clothes during transplanting, pruning and picking; weeds and old crops keep viruses alive between seasons

What to Do

  • Check the field every week and mark plants with mosaic, curling or yellow veins
  • Pull out infected plants early, put them in a bag, and bury or destroy them away from the field; this works best while only a few plants are affected
  • Control the insect carriers: yellow sticky traps for whiteflies and aphids, blue sticky traps for thrips, and neem-based sprays on young plants; ask your agriculture officer before using any insecticide
  • Wash hands with soap and water after touching infected plants, and clean tools before moving to healthy plants
  • Remove weeds in and around the field, because they shelter both the insects and the viruses
  • Give the remaining plants good water and balanced fertiliser so they keep growing

Don't

  • Don't spend money on 'virus cure' sprays; no spray cures a virus-infected plant
  • Don't leave pulled-out plants lying in or beside the field

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many plants show mosaic or curling at an early stage, if a variety sold as resistant is badly infected, or if you need help to confirm which virus is present.

Prevention

  • Grow resistant or tolerant varieties recommended for your area
  • Use certified seed, certified virus-free seed potato and healthy onion sets
  • Raise seedlings under fine insect net and transplant only healthy-looking plants
  • Avoid planting a new crop next to an old, infected crop of the same family
  • Adjust the sowing time if your agriculture department advises it, to avoid the peak of whitefly or aphid activity
  • Destroy crop remains soon after the last picking and keep field edges clean

Problems That Look Similar

  • Tomato leaf curl virus

    Tomato leaves curl upward, stay small and thick with yellow edges; plants are bushy with few fruit; spread by whitefly.

  • Mosaic and leaf curl viruses of cucurbits

    Mosaic, blistered or curled leaves and bumpy, mottled fruit on melons and gourds.

  • Iris yellow spot virus of onion

    Straw-coloured, diamond- or eye-shaped spots on onion leaves and seed stalks, spread by thrips.

  • Herbicide (weedkiller) damage

    Twisted or cupped leaves appearing on many plants at the same time, often on the side nearest a weedkiller spray; new leaves later grow normally.
