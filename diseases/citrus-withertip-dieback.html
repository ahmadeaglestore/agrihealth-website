Withertip (Anthracnose) and Dieback of Citrus

Citrus twigs dry back from the tip, leaves drop and dead shoots turn grey; fruit may get dark tear-stain marks.

Overview

Withertip, or citrus anthracnose, is a fungal disease caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides. The fungus mostly attacks twigs that are already weak. Leaves on the twig fall, the twig dries back from the tip, and dead twigs take on a silvery-grey look. Spores form on dead twigs and dying leaves and are carried by rain, dew and air to new shoots and to fruit, where they can cause reddish-brown or dark 'tear-stain' streaks on the rind and rot after picking. Warm, humid, wet weather favours it.

Twig and branch dieback in citrus often has more than one cause. In Pakistan, citrus decline is common in kinnow orchards; a study in Sargodha district found it in a large share of trees. Researchers link it to poorly drained or waterlogged soil, flood irrigation, old orchards, dead wood left on the trees, intercropping with crops like wheat and berseem, and poor nutrition (affected trees often show zinc-deficiency-like leaves), with fungi such as Lasiodiplodia attacking the weakened wood. So managing dieback means removing dead wood and also fixing the stress that weakened the tree.

Symptoms

  • Leaves on some twigs turn pale and fall
  • Twigs dry back from the tip; dead twigs look silvery grey
  • Dead twigs and dying leaves become covered with tiny dark dots (spore bodies)
  • Small pockets of gum may ooze from young shoots before they die
  • Reddish-brown or dark tear-stain streaks on the fruit rind; fruit may rot after picking
  • In decline, the canopy thins, larger branches dry and the tree weakens season after season

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Colletotrichum gloeosporioides (withertip/anthracnose); other wood fungi such as Lasiodiplodia theobromae attack stressed trees.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores from dead twigs and dying leaves are spread by rain splash, dew and air
  • Weak, stressed trees with a lot of old dead wood are attacked most
  • Waterlogging, poor drainage, flood irrigation and poor nutrition weaken trees
  • Wounds from pruning, storms and insects let fungi in
  • Warm, humid weather with long wet spells favours infection

What to Do

  • In dry weather, cut dead and dying twigs back into healthy green wood; take the cuttings out of the orchard and burn them
  • Clean pruning tools before moving to the next tree
  • Check drainage; do not let water stand around the trunks, and avoid flooding the whole orchard
  • Ask your agriculture officer for a soil and leaf test and the right fertiliser and micronutrients, including zinc
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a protective copper-based spray is needed and the right timing for your area

Don't

  • Don't leave dead wood on the trees or pruned branches lying in the orchard
  • Don't over-irrigate or let water stand around the trunk

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the Citrus Research Institute, Sargodha, if large branches are dying, many trees in the orchard are declining, or gum is oozing from the trunk.

Prevention

  • Remove dead wood from every tree each year
  • Give balanced fertiliser and micronutrients as advised after a soil test
  • Plant on well-drained land and use basin or furrow irrigation instead of flooding the whole orchard
  • Avoid growing heavily watered intercrops such as berseem and wheat under bearing citrus trees
  • Plant healthy, certified nursery plants
  • Water at ground level; avoid sprinklers that wet the leaves and fruit
  • Control pests such as leafminer and psyllid that weaken young shoots

Problems That Look Similar

  • Phytophthora gummosis and foot rot

    Gum oozes from cracked bark near the base of the trunk and the whole tree declines, not just scattered twig tips.

  • Citrus greening (HLB)

    Blotchy, uneven yellow mottling on leaves and small, lopsided, bitter fruit.

  • Citrus canker

    Raised, corky brown spots with yellow halos on leaves, twigs and fruit.
