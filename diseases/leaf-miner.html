Leaf Miners on Vegetables

Thin, white, winding tunnels inside the leaves of melons, gourds, tomato and other vegetables.

Overview

Vegetable leaf miners are tiny black-and-yellow flies. The female pricks the leaf surface and lays eggs inside the leaf. The small, legless, yellowish maggot then eats the green tissue between the upper and lower skin of the leaf, leaving a thin, white, winding (serpentine) tunnel that gets wider as the maggot grows. When fully grown, the maggot usually comes out and drops to the soil, where it forms a small brown, seed-like pupa.

A few tunnels do little harm, but in heavy attack many tunnels join together, leaves dry and fall early, and yield and fruit size can drop; fruit left without leaf cover can get sunburnt. In warm weather the whole life cycle takes only about two weeks. Tiny parasitic wasps often keep leaf miners low, but broad-spectrum sprays used against other pests kill these wasps and can start an outbreak. On tomato, a different and more serious pest, the tomato leafminer (Tuta absoluta), makes wide blotch mines and bores into fruit; it has been found in parts of Pakistan since 2017.

Symptoms

  • Small pale dots on the upper leaf surface where females have fed or laid eggs
  • Thin, white or pale green winding tunnels inside the leaf, getting wider along their length
  • A tiny yellowish maggot can be seen at the wide end of a fresh tunnel when the leaf is held up to the light
  • In heavy attack, tunnels join into large whitish or brown dried patches
  • Mined leaves dry and drop early, and plants lose much of their foliage

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Liriomyza species (tiny leafminer flies such as Liriomyza trifolii and L. sativae); the maggots tunnel inside leaves.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adult flies move between fields and breed on many weeds, flowers and other crops
  • Infested seedlings carry eggs and maggots into new fields
  • Pupae in the soil and old crop plants left after harvest start the next generation
  • Warm weather shortens the life cycle so numbers build up fast
  • Broad-spectrum sprays kill the parasitic wasps that control leaf miners, and leaf miners resist many common insecticides

What to Do

  • Check leaves twice a week and look for fresh tunnels with live maggots inside
  • On small plots and young plants, pick off mined leaves and destroy them away from the field
  • Hang yellow sticky traps among the plants to catch adult flies and see when numbers rise
  • If many tunnels are empty or have dead maggots, natural enemies are probably working; avoid spraying
  • Neem seed kernel extract is a milder option; if damage keeps spreading, ask your agriculture officer whether a spray is needed and which product is approved
  • On tomato, if you see wide blotch mines with a small caterpillar inside, or holes in the fruit, show samples to your agriculture officer, as this may be Tuta absoluta

Don't

  • Don't spray broad-spectrum insecticides again and again; this kills the wasps that control leaf miners and makes outbreaks worse

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if most leaves are mined and drying, or if tomato plants show wide blotch mines and holes in the fruit, which may be the tomato leafminer Tuta absoluta.

Prevention

  • Check seedlings before transplanting and destroy any with tunnels
  • Keep weeds down in and around the field
  • Remove and destroy old crop plants soon after the last picking
  • Raise seedlings under fine insect net
  • Protect parasitic wasps and other natural enemies by spraying only when really needed

Problems That Look Similar

  • Tomato leafminer (Tuta absoluta)

    Wide, irregular blotch mines with dark droppings and a small caterpillar (not a maggot) inside; it also bores into tomato fruit and stems.

  • Citrus leafminer

    Silvery winding tunnels in young citrus leaves made by a small moth caterpillar; it does not attack vegetables.

  • Thrips damage

    Silvery, scraped streaks on the leaf surface, not tunnels inside the leaf.
