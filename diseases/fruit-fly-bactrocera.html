Fruit Flies

Small sting marks on ripening fruit; soft rotten patches with white maggots inside; fruit drop.

Overview

Fruit flies are small reddish-brown flies with yellow markings and clear wings. The female pushes her eggs under the skin of ripening fruit. The white maggots that hatch feed inside the fruit, which rots and often drops. When fully grown, the maggots leave the fruit and turn into pupae in the soil under the tree.

In Pakistan the peach fruit fly (Bactrocera zonata) is the most important species. It attacks guava, mango, peach, apricot, citrus, ber, fig and other fruits, and guava losses can be very high. The flies breed fast in warm weather and slow down in cold weather. Infested fruit cannot be sold, and fruit flies are also a concern for fruit exports.

Symptoms

  • Tiny puncture (sting) marks on the fruit skin, sometimes with oozing sap
  • Soft, discoloured or brown patches around the sting marks
  • Fruit rots from the inside and may drop early
  • White, legless maggots inside the fruit when you cut it open
  • Many fallen fruits under the tree

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Bactrocera zonata (peach fruit fly) and Bactrocera dorsalis (oriental fruit fly).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Females lay eggs in ripening fruit, especially fruit left on the tree too long
  • Fallen and rotting fruit left under trees produces the next generation
  • Maggots turn into pupae in the soil under the trees
  • Moving infested fruit carries the pest to new areas
  • Warm weather speeds up breeding; cold weather slows it

What to Do

  • Collect fallen and infested fruits every day or two and destroy them: bury them deep, or seal them in plastic bags and leave them in the sun
  • Hang methyl eugenol (male lure) traps in the orchard to catch male flies and watch their numbers
  • Ask your agriculture or horticulture officer about bait sprays (protein bait with an approved insecticide applied as spots), which use much less chemical than full-cover sprays
  • Pick fruit at the right maturity and don't leave over-ripe fruit on the tree
  • Work together with neighbouring orchards; area-wide control works much better than one orchard alone

Don't

  • Don't leave fallen fruit under the trees or throw it on field borders
  • Don't send infested fruit to markets or other areas

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture or horticulture officer when many fruits have sting marks or maggots, or when traps catch large numbers of flies; ask about area-wide programmes in your district.

Prevention

  • Keep the orchard clean of fallen and rotten fruit all through the season
  • Hoe or plough the soil under the tree canopy to expose pupae
  • Use male lure traps through the fruiting season
  • Bag fruits on small trees where practical
  • Harvest on time

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango anthracnose

    Black spots and patches on fruit caused by a fungus, with no sting marks and no maggots inside.

  • Cucurbit fruit fly

    A related fly that attacks melons, gourds and other cucurbits rather than tree fruits.

  • Fruit rot from injury or sunburn

    Rot starts from a crack, bruise or sun-scorched side, and there are no maggots inside.
