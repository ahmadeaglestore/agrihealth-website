Mango Malformation (Guchha)

Flower clusters or shoot tips become thick, short and bunched, and set no fruit.

Overview

Mango malformation, called guchha by many growers, is a disease linked mainly to the fungus Fusarium mangiferae. It attacks the buds. Instead of normal long flower clusters (bour) or shoots, the tree makes short, thick, tightly bunched growth. Malformed flower clusters do not give mature fruit.

In Pakistan it is seen in mango orchards of Punjab and Sindh, and common varieties such as Dusehri and Chaunsa can be badly affected. Bunched flowers show up at flowering time in February and March. Bunched shoots (vegetative malformation) are most common on young plants and nursery plants. Affected clusters dry, turn black and can hang on the tree for months, carrying the problem to the next season.

Symptoms

  • Swollen buds that give many small shoots from one point
  • Flower clusters (bour) that are short, thick and heavily branched, like a tight bunch or cauliflower
  • Flowers in the bunch are larger and crowded, and no fruit or only a few tiny fruits set
  • On young plants, shoot tips with very short gaps between leaves and small, narrow, brittle leaves (bunchy top)
  • Bunched clusters dry, turn dark brown to black and stay hanging on the tree

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Fusarium mangiferae (fungus); bud mites can help it enter.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Grafting wood (scions) and nursery plants taken from affected trees carry the fungus to new orchards
  • Pruning tools that are not cleaned can move it from tree to tree
  • Mango bud mites make tiny wounds in buds, which help the fungus get in
  • Dried malformed clusters left on the tree keep the fungus alive for the next season

What to Do

  • Cut off malformed flower clusters and shoots together with a short piece of healthy twig as soon as you see them
  • Collect all cut material and burn or bury it away from the orchard
  • Clean pruning tools between trees
  • Mark affected trees so you do not take grafting wood from them
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a spray for the fungus or bud mites is advised in your area, and which product is approved

Don't

  • Do not leave cut or dried bunches lying under the tree
  • Do not buy plants or grafting wood from nurseries with guchha in their mother trees

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the mango research institute if many trees or nursery plants show bunched growth, or before buying new plants for an orchard.

Prevention

  • Buy healthy grafted plants from a registered, trusted nursery
  • Take grafting wood only from healthy, malformation-free trees
  • Remove and destroy malformed growth every season, before and at flowering
  • Clean and disinfect pruning tools regularly
  • Keep trees strong with proper irrigation and balanced manure
  • Check nursery plants often and pull out any with bunchy tops

Problems That Look Similar

  • Bud mite damage

    Mites can cause bunched shoots on older trees, but they do not turn the flower clusters into thick bunches.

  • Powdery mildew of mango

    Flower clusters are normal in shape but covered with white powder.

  • Mango hoppers

    Flower clusters dry and drop flowers, with small jumping insects and sticky leaves, but the clusters are not thick and bunched.
