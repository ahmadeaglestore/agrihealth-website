Powdery Mildew of Mango

White powder on mango flower clusters, young leaves and tiny fruits, which then drop.

Overview

Powdery mildew is a fungal disease that covers mango flower clusters (bour), young leaves and newly set fruits with a white to greyish powder. Infected flowers and small fruits fall, so fruit set and yield can drop a lot.

The fungus rests in buds and old leaves through winter and becomes active when new flowers appear. In Pakistan the risk is highest at flowering in February and March, especially with cool nights, mild days and cloudy or misty weather. Unlike many fungi, it does not need rain to infect; dry spells with cool nights suit it well.

Symptoms

  • White to greyish powdery patches on flower stalks, flowers and young leaves
  • Powder is often heavier on the young flower stalks and on the underside of young leaves along the veins
  • Flowers turn brown, dry and drop; flower clusters look bare
  • Pea-sized fruits fall in large numbers
  • Young leaves curl and become twisted, and old patches turn purplish-brown
  • Fruits that stay on the tree may get brownish, corky patches

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Oidium mangiferae (fungus, also named Erysiphe quercicola).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The fungus survives the winter in dormant buds and old leaves on the tree
  • Spores are carried by wind from tree to tree
  • Cool nights with mild days, cloud, fog or mist at flowering favour the disease
  • Infected grafting wood can carry it to new areas

What to Do

  • Check flower clusters every few days from the start of flowering
  • Cut off and destroy badly infected clusters and leaves early, when only a few trees are affected
  • Contact your agriculture officer at the first sign of powder; they can advise if a spray is needed, the right time and which product is approved
  • Avoid spraying during full bloom unless the agriculture officer advises it

Don't

  • Do not wait until the flowers have fallen; protection must start early in flowering

When to call an expert: Call your agriculture officer when you first see white powder on the flower clusters, because quick advice at the start of flowering saves the fruit set.

Prevention

  • Remove old infected leaves and malformed or dried panicles after the season
  • Prune to keep the canopy open and well ventilated
  • Keep trees healthy with balanced fertiliser and irrigation
  • Scout regularly from bud burst through fruit set
  • When planting, choose varieties that suffer less in your area on the advice of the research institute

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango anthracnose

    Flowers and leaves get black spots, not white powder.

  • Mango mealybug

    White, cotton-like insects that move, with sticky honeydew, instead of a flat powder.

  • Mango hoppers

    Flowers dry and drop, but there is no white powder and small jumping insects are seen.
