Aphids on Vegetables and Field Crops

Groups of tiny soft green, yellow or black insects under leaves and on shoot tips; leaves curl and get sticky.

Overview

Aphids (tela) are tiny, soft, pear-shaped insects that suck sap from tender shoots and the underside of leaves. They can be green, yellow, grey or nearly black, and most have two small tubes sticking out at the rear end. Most are wingless and move slowly; winged ones appear when colonies get crowded and fly off to new plants. The cotton or melon aphid (Aphis gossypii) attacks melons, cucumbers, gourds, okra, chilli, brinjal, tomato, potato and cotton, and other species such as the green peach aphid are also found on vegetables.

A few aphids do little harm, but colonies grow very fast: females give birth to live young without mating, and in warm weather a new generation can be ready in about a week. Heavy colonies curl and yellow the leaves and stunt new shoots, and their sticky honeydew grows black sooty mould. Some aphids also carry viruses, such as the mosaic viruses of melons and gourds, and can spread them even when their numbers are low. Wheat and mustard have their own aphid guides.

Symptoms

  • Groups of tiny soft insects under young leaves and on growing tips; they do not fly up when the plant is shaken
  • Young leaves curl downwards, crinkle and become cupped
  • Shiny, sticky honeydew on leaves and fruit, often with ants running over the plant
  • Black sooty mould growing on the sticky leaves and fruit
  • Leaves turn yellow, new shoots stay short and young plants are stunted
  • Mosaic, yellow patches or twisted leaves where aphids have spread a virus

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Aphis gossypii (cotton or melon aphid), Myzus persicae (green peach aphid) and other sap-sucking aphids.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Winged aphids fly in from nearby crops and weeds and start new colonies
  • Numbers rise very fast because females give birth without mating, and a new generation takes only about a week in warm weather
  • Too much nitrogen fertiliser makes soft, lush growth that aphids breed on quickly
  • Ants feed on the honeydew and protect aphids from ladybirds and other natural enemies
  • Broad-spectrum sprays kill the natural enemies, and repeated use of the same insecticide group makes aphids resistant, so they come back worse

What to Do

  • Check the underside of leaves and the shoot tips at least twice a week, especially on young plants
  • Hang yellow sticky traps just above the crop to catch winged aphids and see when they arrive
  • On small plots, knock colonies off sturdy plants with a strong spray of water early in the day, or cut off badly infested shoots and destroy them away from the field
  • Look for ladybirds, lacewing and hoverfly larvae, and swollen, golden-brown 'mummy' aphids; where these are common, give them a week or two to work
  • If aphids keep spreading, ask your agriculture officer whether a spray is needed and which product is approved; neem-based or soap-based sprays are milder options and must reach the underside of the leaves

Don't

  • Don't spray broad-spectrum insecticides at the first sight of a few aphids
  • Don't use extra nitrogen fertiliser on a crop that already has aphids

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if aphids keep spreading through the field even after natural enemies appear, or if young plants show mosaic or curled leaves, which may mean a virus is spreading.

Prevention

  • Remove weeds and old crop remains in and around the field soon after harvest; they keep aphids alive between crops
  • Use balanced fertiliser and split nitrogen into small doses through the season rather than one heavy dose
  • Raise vegetable seedlings under fine insect net so they are not attacked, or infected with virus, early
  • Avoid planting a new crop right next to an old crop full of aphids
  • Protect natural enemies by spraying only when really needed
  • Destroy ant nests close to the crop

Problems That Look Similar

  • Whitefly

    Tiny white winged insects that fly up in a cloud when the plant is shaken; aphids are mostly wingless and stay on the plant.

  • Jassid (cotton leafhopper)

    Green, wedge-shaped insects that run sideways and jump away; they do not sit in crowded colonies, and leaf edges turn yellow then red.

  • Mustard aphid

    The same kind of insect on mustard, canola and cabbage-family crops in rabi; see that guide.

  • Wheat aphids

    Aphids on wheat leaves and ears; see the wheat aphid guide.
