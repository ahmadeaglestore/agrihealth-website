Citrus Mealybug

Small white, waxy insects packed between fruits, under the fruit cap and along leaf veins; leaves get sticky.

Overview

The citrus mealybug is a small, soft, oval insect covered with white, mealy wax, with short wax threads around its edge. Adult females are only about three millimetres long. It sucks sap from leaves, young twigs and fruit and likes hidden places: along the veins under leaves, on young twigs, under the fruit cap (button), and where two fruits, or a leaf and a fruit, touch. Females lay hundreds of eggs in white, cottony egg sacs. Besides citrus, it also attacks guava, mango and many other plants.

Feeding weakens the tree, and fruit may drop when mealybugs feed at the fruit stalk. The sticky honeydew they produce grows black sooty mould, which spoils the look and market value of the fruit. Ants feed on the honeydew, protect the mealybugs and carry them from tree to tree. There are several overlapping generations a year, and light attacks are easy to miss because the insects hide. It is a different insect from the large mango mealybug (gadhiri), which climbs tree trunks in winter.

Symptoms

  • White, cotton-like clusters between touching fruits, under the fruit cap and in leaf joints
  • Small, oval, white waxy insects with short wax threads around the edge, along veins under the leaves
  • White cottony egg sacs on twigs, branches and the trunk
  • Sticky honeydew on leaves and fruit, with many ants on the tree
  • Black sooty mould on leaves and fruit
  • Yellowed, curled leaves and weak shoots; fruit may drop or show hard lumps and discoloured patches

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Planococcus citri (citrus mealybug) and related mealybugs.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Ants carry young mealybugs (crawlers) between trees and protect them from natural enemies
  • Crawlers are moved on infested nursery plants, tools, harvest crates and by wind
  • Dense canopies and touching fruit clusters give them hiding places
  • Dust on leaves and broad-spectrum sprays harm the natural enemies that keep mealybugs down
  • Several overlapping generations a year let numbers build up quickly

What to Do

  • Check fruit clusters, under the fruit cap and the underside of leaves on several trees in each part of the orchard
  • Prune out and destroy heavily infested twigs and shoots
  • On small trees, wash mealybugs off with a strong spray of water
  • Control ants: tie a sticky band on a wrap around the trunk (not straight on the bark), prune branches touching the ground, and destroy ant nests near trunks
  • Protect lady beetles and lacewings; ask your agriculture department whether the mealybug lady beetle (Cryptolaemus) is available for release
  • If many fruit clusters are infested, ask your agriculture officer whether a spray is needed, which product is approved and the best timing, as sprays work poorly on hidden mealybugs

Don't

  • Don't spray broad-spectrum insecticides again and again; they kill the lady beetles and tiny wasps that eat mealybugs
  • Don't move crates, tools or plants from an infested orchard without cleaning them

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if mealybugs are on many fruit clusters, fruit is dropping, or sooty mould is spreading across the orchard.

Prevention

  • Plant clean, mealybug-free nursery plants
  • Prune to keep the canopy open and stop branches touching the ground or neighbouring trees
  • Keep ants under control all year
  • Keep dust down on orchard paths
  • Clean equipment and harvest crates before moving them between orchards
  • Check citrus, guava and mango trees regularly, especially fruit clusters

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango mealybug (gadhiri)

    Large, flat mealybugs whose young climb tree trunks in winter, mainly on mango; much bigger than citrus mealybug.

  • Cotton mealybug

    Mealybugs mainly on cotton, okra and vegetables in the field, not packed between citrus fruits.

  • Asian citrus psyllid

    Small brown insects that jump, on new shoots; their young make white waxy threads but do not form cottony clusters on fruit.
