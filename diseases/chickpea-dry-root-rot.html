Dry Root Rot of Chickpea

Chickpea plants dry suddenly at flowering or podding; the main root is black, rotten and brittle and snaps off.

Overview

Dry root rot is a soil-borne fungal disease of chickpea caused by Rhizoctonia bataticola (Macrophomina phaseolina). It usually shows from flowering onwards. The top leaves droop and turn yellow, and within days the whole plant dries to a straw colour. When pulled up, the main root is black and rotten, most of the side roots and fine roots are gone, and the dry root often snaps off in the soil. Tiny black dots (resting bodies of the fungus) can be seen on the root, under the peeling bark and inside the split collar.

The disease is worst when the soil is dry and day temperatures go above about 30 °C during flowering and pod filling. In Pakistan chickpea is a rabi crop, and much of it is grown rain-fed, so late-sown crops that flower and fill pods as the spring heat rises and the soil dries out are most at risk. Research in Karachi found that moisture-stressed chickpea roots were colonised far more by this fungus. The fungus survives for several years in soil and crop debris and attacks many other crops, so it is hard to starve out. It is often confused with chickpea wilt (ukhera).

Symptoms

  • Plants scattered in the field, or in patches, droop at the top, with top leaves and leaf stalks turning yellow first
  • Whole plants dry quickly to a straw colour at flowering or pod filling; lower stems may turn brown
  • Main root is black and rotten, and most side roots and fine roots are missing
  • Dead roots are dry and brittle; the bark shreds and the root tip snaps off when the plant is pulled
  • Tiny black dots on the exposed root, under the peeling bark and inside the collar when split
  • Pods on dried plants stay empty or have small, shrivelled seeds

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Rhizoctonia bataticola (also called Macrophomina phaseolina), a soil-borne fungus.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The fungus lives in soil and crop debris as tiny black resting bodies and survives there for several years
  • Hot weather (days above about 30 °C) and dry soil during flowering and podding greatly increase the disease
  • Late sowing pushes flowering and pod filling into the hot, dry part of spring
  • Plants under water stress in rain-fed fields or sandy soils are attacked more
  • The fungus attacks many other crops and weeds, so short rotations do not remove it; infected debris carries it within and between fields

What to Do

  • Pull up a few drying plants and check the roots: black, rotten, brittle roots with peeling bark and tiny black dots point to dry root rot
  • Split the main root lengthwise: in wilt (ukhera) the root looks healthy outside but has a brown or black streak inside, while in dry root rot the root itself is rotten
  • Where irrigation is available, a light irrigation at flowering and pod filling reduces stress; avoid waterlogging
  • Dried plants cannot be saved; after harvest collect and destroy infected plant remains instead of leaving them in the field
  • Note the affected fields and ask your agriculture officer which tolerant variety, sowing date and seed treatment suit your area

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many plants dry up at flowering or podding, or if you cannot tell whether it is dry root rot or wilt, so the cause can be confirmed and the right variety and sowing plan chosen for next season.

Prevention

  • Sow on time, as recommended for your area, and use an early-maturing variety so that pod filling finishes before the hot weather
  • Save soil moisture: prepare land to hold rain, control weeds early, and give timely irrigation from flowering to pod filling where possible
  • Plough deeply in summer and remove infected crop debris to reduce the fungus in the soil
  • Rotate chickpea with other crops suggested by your agriculture department instead of growing chickpea in the same field year after year
  • Treat seed with an approved fungicide or biological seed treatment recommended by your agriculture department
  • Grow a variety with better tolerance where one is recommended for your area

Problems That Look Similar

  • Chickpea wilt (ukhera)

    Can kill young seedlings too; the whole plant wilts with lower leaves yellowing first, the root looks sound and is harder to pull, and a brown-black streak is seen inside the split root.

  • Collar rot

    Seedlings die in the first weeks after sowing, with a rotten collar covered in white threads and mustard-seed-like bodies.

  • Drought stress

    Plants wilt and dry evenly across dry parts of the field, but roots are white and healthy, with no black rot or black dots.
