Collar Rot of Chickpea

Young chickpea plants yellow and die; stem at soil level rots, with white threads and mustard-seed-like grains.

Overview

Collar rot is a soil-borne fungal disease of young chickpea caused by Sclerotium rolfsii. It appears early, mostly within about six weeks after sowing. Seedlings turn yellow, dry up and die, and dead plants are seen scattered or in small patches across the field. The 'collar', where the stem meets the root, becomes soft, slightly pinched and rotten, and is covered with white, thread-like fungal growth. Small round grains like mustard seeds, first white and then light brown to dark brown, form on this white growth and on the soil around it.

The fungus likes warm temperatures and very moist soil, so collar rot is worst when chickpea is sown into wet soil while it is still warm, early in the rabi season, or when seedlings stand in excess moisture. Undecomposed crop residues and organic matter on the soil surface help the fungus build up. Its mustard-seed-like resting bodies survive for several years near the soil surface, and the fungus attacks hundreds of other plants, including tomato, chilli, potato and cotton. Under favourable conditions it can kill a large share of seedlings.

Symptoms

  • Young plants turn slightly yellow, then dry up and die, mostly in the first six weeks after sowing
  • Dead seedlings scattered across the field or in small patches
  • The collar (stem at soil level) is soft, slightly pinched and rotting, brown to whitish
  • White, cottony or thread-like fungal growth on the collar and root, often spreading onto the soil
  • Small, round, mustard-seed-like grains, white at first and then light to dark brown, on the white growth and on nearby soil
  • The main root of fully dried seedlings is covered with a white fungal coat

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Sclerotium rolfsii (Athelia rolfsii), a soil-borne fungus with mustard-seed-like resting bodies.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Mustard-seed-like resting bodies survive in soil and crop debris for several years, mostly near the surface
  • High soil moisture at sowing and excess water around seedlings favour the disease, especially in warm weather
  • Undecomposed crop residues, weeds and organic matter on the soil surface feed the fungus
  • The fungus spreads from plant to plant by its white threads, and between fields with infested soil, water and infected plant material
  • It attacks many crops and weeds, so it builds up in fields with susceptible crops grown again and again

What to Do

  • Pull up a few dying seedlings and look at the stem at soil level for rot, white threads and mustard-seed-like grains
  • Remove dying and dead seedlings with the soil around them and destroy them away from the field
  • Avoid more irrigation until the topsoil has dried, and drain any standing water
  • Re-sow large gaps only after the soil has dried enough, and ask your agriculture officer whether seed treatment is needed for re-sowing

Don't

  • Don't sow chickpea into very wet soil or give heavy irrigation to young seedlings
  • Don't leave undecomposed crop residue on the surface at sowing

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many seedlings die in the first weeks after sowing, or if collar rot returns in the same field every year, so seed treatment, sowing time and rotation can be planned.

Prevention

  • Plough deeply in summer to bury the resting bodies deep in the soil, where they die faster
  • Remove and destroy crop residues, weeds and undecomposed organic matter before land preparation and after harvest
  • Sow when soil moisture is right, not into wet soil, and protect seedlings from standing water with good levelling and drainage
  • Treat seed with an approved fungicide or biological seed treatment recommended by your agriculture department
  • Rotate with cereal crops such as maize, which are not attacked, in fields where collar rot was common

Problems That Look Similar

  • Chickpea wilt (ukhera)

    Seedlings collapse while staying dull green, and the root has a brown-black streak inside; there is no white growth or mustard-seed-like grains at the collar.

  • Dry root rot

    Comes later, at flowering and podding in hot, dry soil; roots are black and brittle with tiny black dots, not white threads.

  • White mold (Sclerotinia rot)

    White, cottony mould in cool, damp weather later in the season, with large black hard bodies, not small mustard-seed-like grains.
