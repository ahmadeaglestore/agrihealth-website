Powdery Mildew of Chilli

Pale yellow patches on top of chilli leaves, with white powder mostly underneath; leaves then fall.

Overview

Powdery mildew of chilli is caused by the fungus Leveillula taurica. Unlike most powdery mildews, it grows mostly inside the leaf, so the white powder is seen mainly on the underside of the leaf, while the upper side shows pale yellow patches. The same fungus can also attack tomato, brinjal, onion, cotton and many weeds.

It often starts on older leaves just before or at fruit set, but can appear at any stage. Infected leaves curl and fall, so plants lose many leaves and pods are left open to sunscald, which lowers yield. It can develop in both humid and fairly dry weather; high humidity helps the spores to grow, while very hot weather slows the disease for a while.

Symptoms

  • Pale yellow or light green patches on the upper side of leaves, usually starting on older leaves
  • White to greyish powdery growth on the underside of the leaf, below the yellow patches
  • In bad cases, white powder also shows on the upper side of the leaf
  • Patches turn brown and the leaves may curl upward
  • Affected leaves fall off, starting from the lower part of the plant
  • Pods left bare after leaf fall may get sunscald (pale, papery patches)

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Leveillula taurica (Oidiopsis taurica), a powdery mildew fungus that grows mostly inside the leaf.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores are carried by wind from infected plants to healthy ones
  • In good conditions a new round of infection can start about every week to ten days, so it spreads fast
  • The fungus also lives on tomato, brinjal, onion, cotton and weeds, which give spores to chilli fields
  • Infected nursery plants can carry the disease into a new field before any signs show
  • Warm days with humid periods favour it; very hot weather slows it for a time

What to Do

  • Turn over older leaves and check the underside regularly, especially from flowering onward, and act early
  • Remove and destroy badly infected lower leaves and badly infected plants
  • Ask your agriculture officer early about a fungicide or sulfur-based spray; sprays work best at the first signs and must reach the underside of the leaves
  • Remove weeds in and around the field that can carry the fungus

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if white growth is spreading under many leaves or plants are dropping their leaves quickly.

Prevention

  • Raise or buy healthy nursery plants and check the underside of their leaves before transplanting
  • Keep the field and its borders free of weeds
  • Do not plant new chilli next to old tomato, brinjal or chilli crops that have powdery mildew
  • Give proper spacing so air moves and sprays can cover the leaves well
  • Destroy crop remains after the last picking
  • Grow tolerant varieties if your agriculture department recommends any

Problems That Look Similar

  • Spider mites

    Fine yellow speckles on top of the leaf with tiny moving mites and fine webs underneath, not white powder.

  • Whitefly

    Tiny white insects under the leaves that fly up when the plant is shaken; the white does not rub off like powder.

  • Cercospora leaf spot

    Clear round brown spots with grey centres, not yellow patches with white powder underneath.
