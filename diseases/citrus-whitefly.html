Citrus Whitefly and Blackfly

Tiny white or dark flies on citrus shoots; flat or black spiny young under leaves, sticky leaves and black mould.

Overview

Two related sap-sucking insects attack citrus leaves. The citrus whitefly is a tiny insect with white, powdery wings; its flat, pale, scale-like young stick to the underside of leaves and are hard to see. The citrus blackfly has dark, slate-blue adults; it lays its eggs in small spirals under the leaf, and its young are dark brown to black and spiny, with a white wax fringe at the last stage. Both prefer young leaves of new flushes, where females lay their eggs.

Young and adults suck sap and weaken the tree, and their honeydew grows black sooty mould that blocks sunlight on the leaves. Badly attacked trees give smaller crops of poorer fruit, and sooty fruit ripens late and needs washing before sale. A study in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa found citrus whitefly adults peaked in April at two of three orchards. Tiny parasitic wasps, lady beetles, lacewings and, in rainy weather, fungi that kill the young often keep these insects under control, but broad-spectrum sprays and frequent copper sprays harm these natural enemies and can lead to outbreaks.

Symptoms

  • Tiny white flies rise from young leaves when a branch is shaken (whitefly), or small dark bluish-grey flies (blackfly)
  • Flat, pale, scale-like young stuck under the leaves
  • Blackfly: eggs laid in small spirals under leaves, and black, spiny young with a white fringe
  • Sticky honeydew on the leaves below the insects
  • Black sooty mould on the upper side of leaves and on fruit
  • Weak trees with fewer, smaller fruit that ripen late

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Dialeurodes citri (citrus whitefly) and Aleurocanthus woglumi (citrus blackfly).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adults fly to fresh flushes of new leaves to lay eggs
  • Constant new growth, for example after very heavy cutting back, keeps them breeding
  • Infested nursery plants carry young insects to new orchards
  • Broad-spectrum sprays kill parasitic wasps and predators, and frequent copper sprays reduce the fungi that kill whitefly young
  • Numbers often rise in spring with the new flush

What to Do

  • Check the underside of young leaves in spring and after each new flush
  • Hang yellow sticky traps in the canopy to see when adults are active
  • Prune out heavily infested shoots and water shoots and open up crowded canopies
  • Look for natural enemies such as lady beetles and lacewing larvae, and give them time to work
  • If numbers are high, ask your agriculture officer whether a spray is needed and which product is approved; neem-based sprays are a milder option and must reach the underside of leaves

Don't

  • Don't use broad-spectrum insecticides or copper sprays more often than needed
  • Don't cut trees back very heavily, which causes a lot of soft new growth

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if most young leaves have whitefly or blackfly young under them, or if sooty mould is spreading over the trees and fruit.

Prevention

  • Plant clean nursery plants with no young insects under the leaves
  • Prune lightly and regularly every year instead of very heavy cutting
  • Use balanced fertiliser
  • Protect natural enemies by spraying only when really needed
  • Control ants that feed on honeydew
  • Watch each new flush closely, especially in spring

Problems That Look Similar

  • Whitefly on cotton and vegetables

    A related whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) on field crops and vegetables, which also spreads leaf curl viruses; see that guide.

  • Asian citrus psyllid

    Small brown insects that sit tail-up on new shoots and jump; young are yellow-orange with white waxy threads, not flat scales under leaves.

  • Citrus mealybug

    White waxy insects that crawl and gather between fruits; they do not fly.
