Armyworm (Spodoptera litura)

Groups of tiny caterpillars scrape leaves papery; big caterpillars eat leaves at night and hide in soil.

Overview

Armyworm (Spodoptera litura) is a moth caterpillar that eats the leaves of a very wide range of crops, including cotton, cabbage and cauliflower, tomato, brinjal, okra, chilli, potato, melons and gourds, maize, groundnut, soybean and fodder crops. The moth lays its eggs in clusters under the leaves and covers them with brown, hair-like scales.

Young caterpillars feed together in groups and scrape the leaf, leaving a thin papery skin. Older caterpillars spread out, hide in the soil or under leaves during the day and feed at night and early morning, and they can strip plants quickly. In cotton they also eat into bolls.

Symptoms

  • Clusters of eggs under leaves, covered with brown hairy scales
  • Groups of tiny caterpillars on one leaf, leaving papery, see-through patches
  • Large, irregular holes in leaves; in heavy attack only veins and stems remain
  • Big caterpillars, dark grey, green or brown, with light lines along the body and pairs of dark spots on each segment
  • Caterpillars hiding in the soil or leaf litter at the base of plants during the day
  • Holes and droppings in cotton bolls, cabbage heads and fruits

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Spodoptera litura (caterpillar of a moth; also called tobacco caterpillar or cotton leafworm).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Moths fly between fields and lay many eggs on a wide range of crops and weeds
  • Large numbers of caterpillars can crawl from one field into the next
  • Pupae rest in the soil under the crop
  • Weedy fields and nearby vegetable crops keep populations going
  • Overuse of the same insecticides has made it harder to kill in some areas

What to Do

  • Check the underside of leaves for egg clusters and groups of young caterpillars
  • Pick off and destroy leaves with egg masses or groups of young caterpillars before they spread
  • Use pheromone traps to see when moths are active
  • Look for big caterpillars in the soil around plants in the evening or early morning and destroy them
  • Ask your agriculture officer which spray, if any, is approved; biological options such as NPV or Bt may be available, and young caterpillars are easier to control

Don't

  • Don't wait until caterpillars are big; they eat much more and are harder to kill
  • Don't spray vegetables close to picking; follow the waiting period on the label

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer when you find many egg masses or groups of caterpillars, or when leaves are being stripped over a large area.

Prevention

  • Plough after harvest to expose pupae in the soil to birds and sun
  • Keep fields and borders free of weeds
  • Collect egg masses by hand regularly when moths are active
  • Grow trap crops such as sunflower around the field, as advised locally
  • Protect natural enemies such as birds, spiders and parasitic wasps

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fall armyworm

    In maize, fall armyworm hides deep in the whorl and has four dark dots in a square near the tail end.

  • American bollworm

    Eggs are laid singly, not in hairy clusters, and caterpillars bore into fruits and bolls rather than stripping leaves in groups.

  • Diamondback moth

    In cabbage, these caterpillars are small and pale green, and wriggle and drop on a silk thread when touched.
