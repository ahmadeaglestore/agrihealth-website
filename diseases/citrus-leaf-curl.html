Leaf Curl in Citrus (Causes and Remedies)

Citrus leaves curl or twist, mostly on new flush; caused by insects, mites, water stress or sometimes viruses.

Overview

Leaf curl in citrus is a sign, not one disease, so the first job is to find the cause. The most common causes are insects on the soft new flush. Aphids (tela), such as the black citrus aphid, cotton aphid and spirea aphid, suck sap under young leaves and make them curl towards the stem, with sticky honeydew. The citrus leafminer, a tiny moth caterpillar, makes winding tunnels inside young leaves, which then roll and twist. Nymphs of the Asian citrus psyllid feed on tender shoot tips and leave twisted, notched leaves and white waxy threads; this insect also spreads citrus greening. Mites can make leaves dull, speckled or bronzed, and some tiny mites twist young leaves. Heat and water stress also make leaves fold upwards along the midrib in the hot afternoon.

Some viruses that spread with infected budwood can also crinkle, cup or mottle citrus leaves. Surveys in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa found citrus tristeza virus and citrus variegation virus in many orchards, and variegation virus caused leaf crinkling and cupping in tests. Curl from a virus does not go away when insects are controlled. On mature, healthy trees, curled new flush from aphids or leafminer usually does little harm, and natural enemies often bring aphids down within a few weeks; young trees and nursery plants suffer more. Find the cause before you spray.

Symptoms

  • Young leaves of the new flush curl downwards or towards the stem and crinkle, with groups of small soft insects underneath (aphids)
  • Sticky shine on the leaves, ants running on the shoots and black sooty mould
  • Silvery, winding tunnels inside young leaves, which roll and twist (leafminer)
  • Twisted, notched new leaves with tiny insects and white waxy threads on the shoot tips (psyllid)
  • Leaves dull, speckled, silvery or bronzed, sometimes with fine webbing (mites)
  • Leaves fold upwards along the midrib in the hot afternoon and the soil is dry (water stress)
  • Crinkled, cupped or mottled leaves on some branches with no insects, coming back flush after flush (possible virus)

Causes & Spread

Main cause: No single cause: aphids, citrus leafminer, citrus psyllid, mites, water or heat stress, and some graft-spread viruses.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Aphids and psyllids multiply fast on the soft new flush in spring and autumn
  • The leafminer attacks only tender new leaves and is worst on the summer and autumn flushes
  • Too much nitrogen and repeated pruning give many soft flushes that these pests prefer
  • Hot, dry winds, irregular irrigation and damaged roots make leaves curl from water stress
  • Broad-spectrum sprays kill natural enemies and make aphid and mite problems worse
  • Viruses spread through infected budwood and nursery plants; tristeza virus is also spread by aphids

What to Do

  • Turn over the curled leaves and look with a hand lens for soft insects, tunnels, white waxy threads, tiny mites, or nothing at all
  • Check soil moisture in the root zone; water regularly but do not waterlog
  • On mature trees, leave light aphid or leafminer damage alone; natural enemies such as ladybirds and tiny wasps usually control them
  • On young trees and nursery plants with heavy attack, cut off and destroy badly infested shoots; neem-based or soap-based sprays are milder options, and ask your agriculture officer before using any other spray
  • If you see psyllids, contact your agriculture officer quickly, because they spread citrus greening
  • If no pest is found and some branches keep crinkled or mottled leaves, show them to your agriculture officer for virus checking

Don't

  • Don't spray insecticide before you know the cause
  • Don't cut off healthy branches just because the leaves are curled; damaged leaves still feed the tree

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if you find psyllids, if young trees are badly stunted, or if curled, crinkled or mottled leaves keep coming on some branches with no insects or mites present.

Prevention

  • Plant certified, virus-tested nursery plants from registered nurseries
  • Keep flushes short and even: prune live branches once a year and avoid extra nitrogen late in the season
  • Remove water sprouts and root suckers, where pests breed
  • Water regularly and mulch to avoid water stress
  • Protect natural enemies by spraying only when really needed
  • Control ants, which protect aphids from their enemies

Problems That Look Similar

  • Citrus leafminer

    Curled young leaves with silvery winding tunnels inside them; see the leafminer guide.

  • Asian citrus psyllid

    Twisted new shoots with tiny insects and white waxy threads; this insect spreads citrus greening.

  • Spider mites

    Fine pale speckles, dull or bronzed leaves and sometimes fine webs; the tiny mites are seen with a hand lens.
