Stylar End Rot of Guava

Rot starts at the crown (flower end) of guava fruit, turning dark brown and soft; the fruit shrinks and wrinkles.

Overview

Stylar end rot is a fungal fruit rot of guava that starts at the crown or flower end of the fruit, just below the dried calyx, opposite the stalk. A light brown patch appears there and spreads down the fruit as it ripens, becoming dark brown, soft and water-soaked. The flesh inside turns pulpy and light brown instead of white, and the fruit may smell bad. Later the fruit shrinks, forms ring-like wrinkles and is covered with tiny dark dots (the fungus's fruiting bodies).

Infection happens on the tree, but the rot often shows only after picking, so it causes losses in storage and in the market; losses of up to a tenth of the crop in unmanaged orchards, and higher in some reports from India, have been recorded. Warm weather with long wet spells favours it, and wounded fruit is more easily infected. Dried, rotten (mummified) fruit left on the trees carries the fungus to the next season. Indian guidance links it mainly to fruit that matures before winter.

Symptoms

  • A small light brown or whitish patch just below the crown of unripe or ripening fruit
  • The patch spreads down from the crown end, turning dark brown, soft and water-soaked
  • Flesh inside becomes pulpy and light brown, and the fruit may smell bad
  • The fruit shrinks with ring-like wrinkles and tiny dark dots on the rotten part
  • Affected fruit drops early or rots soon after picking

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Phomopsis psidii (main cause); Botryosphaeria-type fungi can cause a similar rot.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Mummified fruit left on trees and on the ground carries the fungus to the next season
  • Warm weather with long wet spells favours infection
  • Wounds on the fruit let the fungus in more easily
  • Crowded canopies keep fruit damp
  • Hidden infections show only after picking and can spread rot in storage

What to Do

  • Pick off and destroy fruit with crown-end rot, and remove mummified fruit from trees and the ground
  • Prune crowded branches to let in light and air
  • Handle fruit gently at picking and sort out any fruit with crown-end spots before packing
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a protective fungicide spray is needed during fruit growth and which product is approved

Don't

  • Don't pack fruit with crown-end rot together with healthy fruit

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many fruits rot from the crown end on the trees or soon after picking.

Prevention

  • Remove all mummified fruit from the orchard after each harvest
  • Space trees properly and prune every year so the canopy stays open
  • Avoid wounding fruit and control fruit flies and other insects that damage fruit
  • Pick fruit on time and keep picked fruit cool and dry
  • Plant healthy nursery plants

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fruit flies

    Small puncture marks anywhere on the skin and white maggots inside the soft fruit; rot does not start only at the crown.

  • Guava anthracnose

    Sunken dark spots anywhere on the fruit, often with pink or orange spore masses in damp weather.

  • Other fruit rots (Botryodiplodia and similar)

    Soft, watery rot that may start at the stalk or crown and soon turns black with dark fungal growth; a laboratory is needed to tell them apart.
