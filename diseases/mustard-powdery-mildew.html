Powdery Mildew of Mustard

Flat, dusty white powder on mustard leaves, stems and pods, mostly late in the season.

Overview

Powdery mildew is a fungal disease caused by Erysiphe cruciferarum. Small white powdery patches appear on the leaves, then join and cover the whole leaf, the stems and the pods, as if they were dusted with flour. The fungus grows on the surface of the plant and spreads by spores carried in the wind. Unlike most leaf diseases, it does not need rain. Older leaves and stressed plants are attacked more than young, healthy ones. Mustard, canola and cabbage-family vegetables can all get it.

In mustard it usually appears late in the crop, from flowering to pod filling, which in Pakistan's rabi crop means late winter and early spring. It is most common in cool to mild, dry weather with low rainfall; rain can wash spores off the leaves. Heavy, early attack makes leaves yellow and drop early, pods fill poorly and seeds become small and light, with less oil. Studies in India have reported serious losses in some areas, but a light attack that starts only when pods are nearly mature does little harm.

Symptoms

  • Small, round, white powdery spots on older, lower leaves, on either side of the leaf
  • Spots join until the whole leaf looks dusted with white flour or ash
  • The powder rubs off easily with a finger, leaving green or yellowish leaf below
  • White powder on stems and pods, which can turn greyish or dark later
  • Leaves turn yellow, then brown, and drop early
  • Pods fill poorly, with small, shrivelled seed

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Erysiphe cruciferarum (powdery mildew fungus of the cabbage family).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores blow in the wind from plant to plant and from field to field
  • The fungus survives on cabbage-family weeds, volunteer plants and as tiny resting bodies on crop remains
  • Cool to mild, dry weather with low rainfall and low humidity favours it
  • Too much nitrogen, a dense crop and drought stress make plants more likely to be attacked
  • Older leaves are attacked more than young ones, so it usually builds up late in the season

What to Do

  • From flowering onwards, check older leaves, stems and pods for white powder
  • Irrigate on time so plants are not stressed by drought, and don't add extra nitrogen
  • On small plots, remove the lowest, most infected leaves
  • If powder appears early, before pods have filled, and spreads fast, ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide spray is worthwhile and which one is approved; a late, light attack usually does not need spraying

When to call an expert: Ask your agriculture officer for advice if white powder covers many leaves and pods before the seed has filled, as yield and oil content can drop.

Prevention

  • Sow at the recommended time, and use varieties advised for your area
  • Use balanced fertiliser, including potash, as advised; avoid heavy nitrogen
  • Don't sow too thickly; a very dense crop stays stressed and shaded
  • Remove cabbage-family weeds and volunteer mustard plants
  • Destroy infected crop remains after harvest
  • Rotate with crops outside the cabbage family

Problems That Look Similar

  • White rust

    Raised, shiny white blisters under the leaves with yellow spots above, and swollen, twisted flower heads; the powder of white rust comes from inside blisters, not a flat surface layer.

  • Downy mildew

    Yellow patches on top of the leaf with grey fuzzy growth below, usually in damp, foggy weather.

  • Mustard aphid

    Dense colonies of tiny grey-green insects on flower stalks and pods; close up you can see insects, not powder.
