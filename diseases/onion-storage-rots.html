Neck Rot and Storage Rots of Onion

Stored onions go soft at the neck, show grey or black powdery mould under the skin, or rot with a bad smell.

Overview

Onion bulbs can rot in storage because of several fungi and bacteria. Neck rot (Botrytis) makes the neck soft and watery, with grey, dusty mould between the scales; it usually shows several weeks after the onions go into store. Black mould (Aspergillus niger) forms sooty black powder under the dry outer skins, often along the veins and at the neck. Blue-green mould (Penicillium) makes green, dusty patches. Bacterial soft rot turns the inner scales brown, watery and foul-smelling, even when the bulb looks sound from outside.

Most of these problems start in the field or at harvest: the germs live in soil and onion debris, neck rot can also come with seed, and many enter through bruises, cuts or a neck that is still wet and thick at harvest. Black mould and bacterial rots like heat above about 30 °C with high humidity, so they matter a lot in Pakistan, where much of the onion crop is lifted and stored in hot weather (in Punjab the winter crop is transplanted in December–January). Good curing, gentle handling and dry, airy storage prevent most losses.

Symptoms

  • Neck of the bulb soft, sunken and watery; the rot moves down into the bulb as a soft brown rot
  • Grey, dusty mould between the scales near the neck, sometimes with small black, hard bodies under the skins (neck rot)
  • Sooty black powder under the dry outer skins, in streaks along the veins or at the neck; bulbs later shrivel (black mould)
  • Green, dusty or felt-like mould on the scales and at the neck (blue-green mould)
  • Bulb looks fine from outside, but inner scales are brown, watery and soft, with a strong bad smell when cut or squeezed (bacterial soft rot)
  • Rot spreads from one bulb to those touching it, leaving wet patches and bad smell in sacks or heaps

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Botrytis aclada and B. allii (neck rot), Aspergillus niger (black mould), Penicillium species (blue-green mould) and soft-rot bacteria such as Pectobacterium carotovorum.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Germs live in soil and on onion debris; neck rot fungus is also carried on seed
  • Neck rot infects in the field during moist weather without showing, then grows if the neck is still green and wet at harvest
  • Bruises, cuts and sunburnt or damaged skins let black mould and soft-rot bacteria enter
  • Rain or heavy irrigation close to harvest, poor curing and lifting in wet weather increase rotting
  • Heat with high humidity, poor air movement and deep heaps or tightly packed sacks in store make all these rots worse
  • Rotting bulbs pass the rot to healthy bulbs touching them

What to Do

  • Check stored onions every week or two; remove soft, smelly or mouldy bulbs at once and keep them away from the store
  • Spread out bulbs from wet or warm sacks and heaps so they can dry and air can move between them
  • Sell or use soon any lot that is starting to rot instead of keeping it longer
  • Improve ventilation in the store: open vents at cool, dry times and avoid stacking sacks against walls or on a damp floor
  • Note which field and variety rotted most; ask your agriculture officer for advice on curing, storage and seed treatment for the next crop

Don't

  • Don't store onions that are bruised, cut, thick-necked or not fully dry
  • Don't throw or drop sacks; bruising lets rot germs in
  • Don't keep onions in deep heaps or closed, airless rooms

When to call an expert: Ask your agriculture officer for help if many stored onions rot within the first few weeks, or if rot keeps coming back in your store every season, so the cause can be checked and curing and storage improved.

Prevention

  • Use treated seed from a trusted source, since neck rot can come with seed
  • Stop irrigating as the crop matures, as advised locally, and harvest in dry weather when most tops have fallen over; don't leave a mature crop lying in the field too long
  • Cure the bulbs well before storage: dry them in a shaded, airy place, turning them, until necks are tight and outer skins are dry and papery; protect them from rain
  • Handle bulbs gently at lifting, topping, grading and loading; remove damaged, soft and thick-necked bulbs before storage
  • Store only dry, cured bulbs in a clean, dry, well-ventilated store, on slatted racks or in mesh bags, in thin layers rather than deep heaps
  • Keep the store as cool and dry as you can, and clean out old onion waste before the new crop comes in
  • Rotate onion fields and destroy rotten and culled onions away from fields

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fusarium basal rot

    Rot starts at the root plate at the bottom of the bulb, often with white or pinkish mould and leaves yellowing in the field; neck rot starts at the top.

  • Drying, sprouting and rooting in store

    Not a disease: bulbs lose weight, shrivel, or push out green shoots and roots when the store is warm and humid; there is no mould or bad smell.
