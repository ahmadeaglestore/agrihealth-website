Whitefly

Tiny white flies under leaves that fly up when touched; sticky leaves, black mould and yellowing.

Overview

Whitefly is a tiny sap-sucking insect with white wings, about the size of a pinhead. Adults and their flat, scale-like young sit on the underside of leaves. It feeds on cotton and many vegetables, including tomato, okra, brinjal, chilli, potato, melons and gourds, and it also lives on many weeds.

Feeding weakens the plant, and the sticky honeydew it leaves behind grows black sooty mould and spoils cotton lint. Even more serious, whitefly carries the viruses that cause cotton leaf curl, tomato leaf curl, chilli leaf curl and okra yellow vein mosaic. Numbers build up as the weather warms and are usually highest in mid to late summer, in the kharif season.

Symptoms

  • Clouds of tiny white flies rise from the plant when you shake it
  • Flat, pale yellow, scale-like young insects stuck under the leaves
  • Leaves become sticky and shiny with honeydew
  • Black sooty mould on the sticky leaves and on open cotton bolls
  • Small yellow spots on leaves that join up; leaves may dry and drop early
  • Plants grow poorly; in cotton, squares and bolls may shed
  • Curled leaves, thick veins or yellow mosaic where whitefly has spread a virus

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Bemisia tabaci (sap-sucking whitefly that also spreads leaf curl viruses).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adults fly from field to field, and wind carries them further
  • Weeds, vegetables and crop leftovers keep whiteflies alive between seasons
  • Warm weather lets numbers grow quickly
  • Repeated sprays of the same insecticide group make whiteflies harder to kill (resistance)
  • Broad-spectrum sprays kill the small wasps and predators that normally keep whitefly down

What to Do

  • Check the underside of leaves at the field edge and inside the field at least twice a week
  • Hang yellow sticky traps to see when numbers are rising
  • Remove weeds in and around the field, and pull out young plants with leaf curl or yellow mosaic early if your agriculture officer advises it
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether numbers are high enough to need a spray and which product is approved; neem-based sprays can help on small plots
  • If a spray is advised, make sure it reaches the underside of the leaves, where whiteflies sit

Don't

  • Don't spray every week 'just in case'; it kills natural enemies and builds resistance
  • Don't mix several insecticides together on your own

When to call an expert: Call your agriculture officer when you see many whiteflies on most leaves, sticky leaves spreading through the field, or leaf curl or yellow mosaic on young plants.

Prevention

  • Grow varieties recommended for your area, including leaf-curl tolerant cotton where available
  • Sow at the recommended time and keep fields and borders free of weeds
  • Avoid planting cotton next to melons, vegetables or other crops full of whitefly
  • Destroy crop leftovers and regrowth soon after harvest
  • Protect natural enemies such as tiny parasitic wasps, lady beetles and lacewings by avoiding unnecessary sprays
  • Raise vegetable seedlings under fine insect net so they are not infected with virus early

Problems That Look Similar

  • Jassid (cotton leafhopper)

    Jassids are green and wedge-shaped and run sideways; they do not rise in white clouds when the plant is shaken.

  • Cotton mealybug

    Mealybugs are bigger, covered in white wax like cotton wool, and crawl slowly; they do not fly.

  • Aphids

    Aphids are soft, green or black insects in groups on shoots and under leaves; they have no white wings.
