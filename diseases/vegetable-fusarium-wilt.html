Fusarium and Verticillium Wilt of Vegetables

Lower leaves turn yellow, often on one side, plants wilt slowly, and the cut stem shows brown streaks inside.

Overview

Fusarium wilt and Verticillium wilt are caused by soil fungi that enter through the roots and grow inside the water pipes of the stem. The plant cannot move enough water, so leaves yellow and wilt, often on one side of the plant or on one runner first. The two diseases look so alike in the field that only a laboratory can tell them apart, and they are managed in the same way.

Each form of Fusarium attacks only its own crop (for example tomato or watermelon), while Verticillium attacks many crops. Fusarium is worse in warm weather and Verticillium in cooler weather. A study in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa found Fusarium wilt of tomato across the districts surveyed, and growers said it was increasing every year. The fungi live in soil for many years, so resistant varieties, grafting and long rotation are the main defences; sprays do not cure wilted plants.

Symptoms

  • The lowest, oldest leaves turn yellow first, often on one side of the plant, on one branch or on one half of a leaf
  • Leaves wilt in the hottest part of the day, then dry and hang on the plant or drop
  • Yellowing and wilting move up the plant over days to weeks; plants become stunted and may die
  • Split the lower stem lengthwise: the water pipes show brown or tan streaks, but the stem is not soft or rotten
  • Melons and other cucurbits: one runner yellows and wilts first, then the others collapse; there may be reddish gum at the base
  • Fruit stays small and poor; fruit exposed by leaf loss can get sunscald
  • No milky threads come out when a cut stem is put in water (unlike bacterial wilt)

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Fusarium oxysporum (crop-specific forms, e.g. f. sp. lycopersici on tomato, f. sp. niveum on watermelon) and Verticillium dahliae; soil-borne fungi.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The fungi survive in soil for many years (about 5–10 years) and in infected crop remains
  • Infected nursery plants, nursery soil and seed bring them to new fields
  • Soil on tools, machinery, reused stakes and shoes, and flood water, move them within and between fields
  • Root wounds from root-knot nematodes, hoeing and transplanting help the fungi get in
  • Growing the same crop in the same field year after year builds up the fungus

What to Do

  • Split the lower stem of a wilting plant to look for brown streaks, and do the stem-in-water test to rule out bacterial wilt
  • Pull out wilted plants with their roots and take them out of the field; do not leave them lying there or compost them
  • Mark the affected patches, work in them last, and clean tools and shoes afterwards
  • Ask your agriculture officer to confirm the cause and advise on resistant varieties or grafted plants for the next crop; chemical control is not practical for these wilts

Don't

  • Don't spray fungicides on wilting plants hoping to save them
  • Don't move soil or plant remains from an affected field to clean fields

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if several plants wilt with brown streaks inside the stem, so the cause (Fusarium, Verticillium or bacterial wilt) can be confirmed before you choose varieties for the next crop.

Prevention

  • Grow resistant varieties; tomato seed packets marked V, F or VF show resistance (VFN also resists root-knot nematodes)
  • In fields with a history of wilt, use plants grafted onto resistant rootstocks where available
  • Rotate away from the affected crop for at least five years; longer is better for watermelon
  • Raise the nursery in clean soil and buy plants only from clean nurseries
  • Solarise nursery beds or small plots before planting: cover moist soil with clear plastic sheet during the hottest weeks
  • Control root-knot nematodes and avoid wounding roots
  • Remove and destroy all crop remains, including roots, after harvest

Problems That Look Similar

  • Bacterial wilt

    Plants wilt suddenly while still green, and a cut stem in water releases milky threads.

  • Root-knot nematode

    Roots have knots or swellings; the inside of the stem stays clean.

  • Phytophthora blight of chilli

    Dark rot at the stem base at soil level after heavy watering, not brown streaks higher up inside the stem.
