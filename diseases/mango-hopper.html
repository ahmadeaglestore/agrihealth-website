Mango Hoppers

Small wedge-shaped insects jump on the flowers; flowers dry and drop and leaves turn sticky and black.

Overview

Mango hoppers (called tela by many growers) are small, wedge-shaped insects that suck sap from flower clusters, tender shoots and leaves. Both young (nymphs) and adults feed. The flowers wither and drop, and young fruits fall. The insects also pass a sweet sticky liquid (honeydew) on which black sooty mould grows.

Hoppers live in the tree all year, hiding in bark cracks, but their numbers jump at flowering. They are most damaging from February to April, when they lay eggs in flower stalks and tender shoots. Old, dense, shady orchards with crowded branches are most at risk.

Symptoms

  • Many small (about half a centimetre) wedge-shaped insects that jump or fly a short distance when the branch is shaken
  • Pale green, wingless nymphs crowded on flower stalks and tender shoots
  • Flower clusters wither, turn brown and flowers drop; few fruits set
  • Sticky, shiny drops (honeydew) on leaves and flowers
  • Black sooty mould covering leaves, flowers and fruit
  • A light ticking or rustling sound in the canopy when hoppers are many

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Leafhoppers: Idioscopus clypealis, Idioscopus nitidulus, Amritodus atkinsoni.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adults fly between trees and nearby orchards
  • Numbers rise fast at flowering when females lay eggs inside flower stalks and tender shoots
  • Shade and high humidity in dense, unpruned orchards favour them
  • Old neglected orchards and close planting give hoppers good shelter

What to Do

  • From the start of flowering, shake a few branches over a cloth or look closely at flower clusters to check for hoppers
  • Contact your agriculture officer if many hoppers are seen on the flower clusters; they can advise on the right time to spray and which product is approved
  • Neem-based sprays can help when numbers are still low
  • If spraying is needed, avoid the hours when bees are visiting the flowers

Don't

  • Do not spray at full bloom unless advised, as it can harm the bees that pollinate mango
  • Do not wait until flowers have dried; damage happens early in flowering

When to call an expert: Call your agriculture officer at flowering time if you see many hoppers on each flower cluster or if flowers are drying and leaves are turning sticky.

Prevention

  • Prune crowded and overlapping branches after harvest or in winter to let in light and air
  • Keep the orchard clean: remove weeds and plough lightly between rows
  • Avoid close planting in new orchards
  • Avoid too much nitrogen fertiliser, which gives soft, attractive growth
  • Protect natural enemies such as spiders and lacewings by avoiding needless sprays

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango mealybug

    Insects are flat, white and cotton-covered and crawl slowly; they do not jump.

  • Powdery mildew of mango

    Flowers drop too, but the clusters are covered with white powder and no insects jump.

  • Mango anthracnose

    Flowers turn black with spots after rain, with no insects or honeydew.
