Leaf Mold of Tomato

Pale yellow patches on top of tomato leaves with olive-brown velvety mould underneath, mostly in tunnels.

Overview

Leaf mold is a fungal disease of tomato leaves. It is mainly a problem in plastic tunnels and greenhouses, where the air stays very humid, but it can also appear in the open field during long damp spells. It usually starts on the older, lower leaves and moves upward.

The fungus needs very high humidity (above about 85%) and mild to warm temperatures of about 20–25 °C. Where tomatoes are grown in closed plastic tunnels, as in much off-season tomato growing in Pakistan, trapped moisture and dripping at night make it spread, so good ventilation is the key control. Badly affected plants lose many leaves and yield falls.

Symptoms

  • Pale green or yellowish patches without a clear edge on the upper side of older leaves
  • Olive-green to brown, velvety (furry) mould on the underside of the leaf, just below the yellow patches
  • The patches turn bright yellow and then brown
  • Badly affected leaves curl, wither and drop, starting from the bottom of the plant
  • Sometimes flowers die, and fruit can get a dark, leathery rot at the stem end

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Passalora fulva (also called Fulvia fulva or Cladosporium fulvum), a fungus.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores move easily with air, water splash, tools, workers' clothes and insects
  • Relative humidity above about 85% and long periods of wet leaves, as in closed tunnels
  • The fungus survives on infected crop remains and in the tunnel for six months to a year, and can come in on seed
  • Dense, unpruned plants and night-time condensation (water dripping inside the plastic) make it worse
  • New spores form within about 10–12 days, so the disease can build up quickly during one season

What to Do

  • Open the tunnel sides or ends during the day to let humid air out, especially in the morning after cold nights
  • Remove lower leaves that have mould and take them out of the tunnel in a bag
  • Prune extra side shoots and stake or tie plants so air can move between them
  • Water at the base (drip or furrow) and avoid wetting the leaves
  • If the mould keeps spreading, ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide is needed and which one is approved

Don't

  • Don't keep tunnels closed all day in mild weather
  • Don't leave infected leaves lying on the tunnel floor

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if the mould keeps spreading up the plants even with good ventilation, or if flowers and fruit are being affected.

Prevention

  • Grow a variety resistant to leaf mold where available; ask your seed supplier
  • Use certified, healthy seed
  • Space plants well and plan good ventilation when setting up tunnels
  • After the season, remove all crop remains and clean the tunnel frame, plastic and tools
  • Avoid growing tomato in the same tunnel soil every year; move tunnels or rotate crops where possible

Problems That Look Similar

  • Late blight

    Large dark, wet-looking patches that spread very fast; any growth underneath is thin and white, not olive-brown and velvety.

  • Powdery mildew

    Also gives yellow patches on top, but the growth underneath is white and powdery, not olive-brown and velvety.

  • Septoria leaf spot

    Small round spots with grey centres and black dots; no velvety mould under the leaf.
