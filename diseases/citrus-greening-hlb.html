Citrus Greening (Huanglongbing, HLB)

Blotchy yellow-green leaves that differ on each side of the midrib; small, lopsided, bitter fruit.

Overview

Citrus greening, also called huanglongbing or HLB, is a bacterial disease that lives in the food-carrying tissue of the tree. It is spread by a small insect, the Asian citrus psyllid, and by grafting with infected budwood. There is no cure, and infected trees slowly decline.

Greening is present in Punjab. A survey of orchards around Faisalabad, Kot Momin, Bhalwal and Multan found it in every area, with more disease where psyllids were more common. Its leaf mottle is easy to confuse with zinc deficiency, so check leaves carefully.

Symptoms

  • Blotchy yellow and green mottling on leaves that looks different on the left and right side of the midrib
  • Yellow shoots, or one yellow branch on an otherwise green tree
  • Thick, leathery leaves, sometimes with yellow or corky veins; small, upright leaves
  • Fruit small and lopsided, staying green at the bottom end, with sour, bitter juice
  • Seeds small, brown and poorly formed, and brown-stained strands inside the fruit
  • Leaf and fruit drop, twig die-back and slow decline of the whole tree

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (bacterium), spread by the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The Asian citrus psyllid picks up the bacterium from sick trees and carries it to healthy trees
  • Grafting or budding with budwood from infected trees
  • Buying plants from unknown or uncertified nurseries
  • More psyllids mean more greening; psyllids peak during the spring and autumn flush
  • Curry leaf (Murraya) plants near orchards help psyllids multiply

What to Do

  • Do the pen test: on a mottled leaf, draw a circle on each side of the midrib; if the two sides look different, suspect greening
  • Check new flush for psyllids and their yellow-orange young
  • Mark suspected trees and ask your agriculture department or citrus institute to test them
  • If greening is confirmed, remove infected trees as your agriculture officer advises, so they do not keep feeding psyllids
  • Keep the other trees well fed and watered; this helps them cope but does not cure greening

Don't

  • Don't take budwood or make new plants from suspected trees
  • Don't assume it is only zinc deficiency; fertiliser will not cure greening

When to call an expert: Report suspected greening to your agriculture department or citrus research institute. Only a laboratory test can confirm it, and quick removal of infected trees protects the rest of the orchard.

Prevention

  • Plant only certified, disease-free nursery plants
  • Control psyllids on new flush, especially in spring and autumn, following agriculture department advice
  • Remove curry leaf (Murraya) plants near orchards
  • Inspect trees regularly and remove confirmed infected trees quickly
  • Protect natural enemies of the psyllid, such as the tiny wasp Tamarixia radiata and hover fly larvae

Problems That Look Similar

  • Zinc deficiency

    Yellowing between green veins that looks the same on both sides of the midrib; fruit is usually normal and new leaves green up after zinc is supplied.

  • Phytophthora root rot

    The whole tree looks pale with a thin canopy, roots are rotten and there may be gum on the trunk, but no blotchy mottle.
