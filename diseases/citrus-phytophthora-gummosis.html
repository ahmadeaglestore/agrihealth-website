Phytophthora Gummosis, Foot Rot & Root Rot of Citrus

Gum oozing from cracked bark near the trunk base; bark dies and the tree turns yellow and thin.

Overview

Phytophthora water moulds are present in almost all citrus orchards. When the trunk base stays wet, they attack the bark (gummosis or foot rot) and the small feeder roots (root rot). The tree slowly turns pale, gives less fruit, and can die if the bark is killed all around the trunk.

In Punjab most kinnow trees are grafted on rough lemon (jatti khatti) rootstock, which is susceptible to Phytophthora root and collar rot, and most orchards are flood-irrigated. Water standing against the trunk, poor drainage and soil heaped over the bud union greatly raise the risk.

Symptoms

  • Gum oozing from small bark cracks near the soil line or bud union (rain may wash it away)
  • Bark under the gum turns dark brown, dries, cracks and peels off
  • The dead patch spreads around the trunk and can girdle it
  • Leaves turn pale green or yellow and drop, and the canopy becomes thin
  • Feeder roots rot; their soft outer skin slips off and leaves a thin thread
  • Poor growth, twig die-back and a smaller crop; fruits near the ground may get brown rot

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Phytophthora species (e.g. P. citrophthora, P. nicotianae / P. parasitica), soil water moulds.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Swimming spores are carried by irrigation and rain water and splash onto the trunk
  • Water standing against the trunk in flood irrigation
  • Poor drainage, heavy soils and over-watering
  • Soil heaped over the bud union, or trees planted too deep
  • Susceptible rootstocks such as rough lemon, and wounds on the trunk or roots

What to Do

  • Check the lower trunk and crown of every tree for gum
  • Change irrigation so water does not touch the trunk; use a double-ring basin that keeps a dry ring around the trunk
  • Pull soil away from the trunk base so the bud union is above the soil and can dry
  • Improve drainage in waterlogged parts of the orchard
  • Ask your agriculture officer about scraping out the diseased bark, treating the wound and using an approved fungicide

Don't

  • Don't let irrigation water stand against the trunk
  • Don't injure the trunk or roots with tools while hoeing or weeding

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if gum and dead bark are found on several trees, or if the trunk is dead around half its width or more; such trees may be better replaced.

Prevention

  • Plant on a raised bed or ridge, not too deep, with the bud union well above the soil
  • For new plantings, use tolerant rootstocks recommended for your area
  • Use a double-ring basin, furrow or drip irrigation that keeps the trunk dry
  • Avoid over-watering and correct drainage problems
  • Avoid wounds on the trunk and roots
  • Collect and destroy fallen diseased fruits and leaves
  • Inspect trees for gum several times a year

Problems That Look Similar

  • Citrus greening

    Blotchy mottle that differs on each side of the midrib and lopsided fruit, with no gum or dead bark at the trunk base.

  • Waterlogging or salt damage

    Similar yellowing and root loss but no gum on the trunk; a laboratory test may be needed to confirm Phytophthora.
