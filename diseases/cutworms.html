Cutworms

Young plants found cut through at the base in the morning; fat grey-brown caterpillars curl up in the soil nearby.

Overview

Cutworms (tooka sundi) are the caterpillars of dark brown moths that fly at night. Young caterpillars feed on leaves and make small holes or scrape the leaf surface. Older caterpillars are plump, smooth and greasy-looking, grey-brown to nearly black, and up to about four to five centimetres long; when disturbed they curl into a 'C' shape. By day they hide in the soil near the plant base, under clods or in cracks. At night they come out and cut young plants at or just below the soil, sometimes dragging them into their hiding place. One caterpillar can cut several plants along a row.

Cutworms attack many crops, including potato, tomato, chilli, onion, cabbage and cauliflower, chickpea, maize, wheat and cotton, and the black cutworm is reported damaging wheat and vegetables in Pakistan. They also eat holes in potato tubers. Damage is worst on young seedlings and new transplants, and gaps appear in the field almost overnight. A study at Peshawar found moths flying from late February, with peaks in March, mid-April and mid-May, while caterpillars and pupae did not survive the hot, dry June. Weedy fields, low-lying wet patches and light, sandy soil in dry years are most at risk.

Symptoms

  • Small holes or scraped patches on the lower leaves of young plants (young caterpillars)
  • Seedlings and young transplants found cut through at or just below the soil surface in the morning
  • Cut plants lying on the ground, or partly pulled into a hole in the soil
  • Gaps in rows, often several cut plants one after another along the same row
  • Plump, smooth, greasy grey-brown to black caterpillars curled like a 'C', found by digging lightly around the base of cut plants
  • Potato tubers with irregular holes eaten into them
  • Remaining plants may wilt where the stem has been partly eaten through

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Caterpillars of night-flying moths: Agrotis ipsilon (black or greasy cutworm) and Agrotis segetum (common cutworm or turnip moth).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Moths fly in at night and lay eggs on low leaves, weeds and plant debris near the ground
  • Weedy fields and fields where grass, fodder or weeds were destroyed shortly before sowing have more caterpillars
  • Low-lying patches that were recently flooded or stay wet attract egg-laying moths
  • Light, sandy soils in hot, dry years suffer more damage; rain or irrigation can kill many young caterpillars
  • Cool weather slows crop growth, so seedlings stay small and easy to cut for longer

What to Do

  • In the early morning, check rows for freshly cut plants and dig lightly in the soil around them; collect and destroy the caterpillars by hand, especially on small plots
  • Repeat hand picking in the evening and early morning for a few days, also checking soil cracks and under clods
  • Where the crop allows, irrigate the infested field; water brings caterpillars to the surface and kills many young ones, and birds pick them up
  • Fill gaps by re-sowing or transplanting once the caterpillars are under control
  • If many plants are being cut, ask your agriculture officer whether an approved bait or insecticide is needed, how to apply it near the plant base, and at what time of day

Don't

  • Don't sow or transplant straight after ploughing in heavy weed or grass growth; give it time to dry out first
  • Don't spray the whole crop canopy for cutworms; the caterpillars hide in the soil, so leaf sprays mostly miss them

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if cut plants keep appearing over a large part of the field even after hand picking and irrigation, or if your fields are attacked every year.

Prevention

  • Plough deeply in summer before sowing to expose caterpillars and pupae to sun and birds
  • Keep fields and field edges free of weeds, and destroy weeds and grass at least two weeks before sowing
  • Level the field and improve drainage of low, wet patches
  • Put up bird perches in the field so birds can feed on caterpillars
  • Use light traps or pheromone traps for moths to see when they are active, and check young crops more often at those times
  • Check seedlings and new transplants every few days for the first weeks after sowing or transplanting

Problems That Look Similar

  • Termites

    Plants dry up and pull out easily with roots or stem base eaten, often with mud tunnels and small white insects; plants are not cut cleanly at the soil line overnight.

  • Damping-off

    Seedling stems turn thin, soft and rotten at the soil line and fall over in patches, especially in wet beds; no caterpillars in the soil.

  • Leaf-eating caterpillars

    Caterpillars eat leaves on the plant and stay on it; they do not hide in the soil by day or cut plants through at the base.
