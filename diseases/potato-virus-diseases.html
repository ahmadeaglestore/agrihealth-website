Virus Diseases of Potato (Leafroll, PVY and Mosaic)

Potato leaves rolled upward, stiff and leathery, or mottled and crinkled; plants stunted and tubers small.

Overview

Several viruses infect potato. The most important are potato leafroll virus (PLRV), which makes the leaves roll and become stiff, and potato virus Y (PVY), which causes mosaic, crinkling and dead streaks. Potato virus X (PVX) often causes only mild mottling, but it makes PVY worse when both are present. These viruses live inside the tubers, so a plant grown from an infected seed tuber is sick from the start and its tubers carry the virus to the next crop. This is why seed that is kept and replanted for several seasons gives lower and lower yields.

In Pakistan, a study found much more leafroll in uncertified market seed than in certified seed, and the Federal Seed Certification and Registration Department tests seed crops for PLRV, PVX, PVY and other viruses. In the field, aphids spread these viruses, mainly when aphid numbers rise in the warmer part of each potato season. Using certified seed, removing sick plants early and protecting seed crops from aphids are the main ways to keep viruses down. No spray can cure an infected plant.

Symptoms

  • Leafroll from infected seed: the lower leaves roll upward along the edges, become stiff, thick and leathery, and rustle like paper when touched
  • Leafroll plants are pale or yellowish, stand stiffly upright and stay short; some leaves show a purple or reddish tinge
  • Leafroll caught in the current season: only the top leaves roll and look pale or reddish
  • Mosaic: light and dark green mottling, with crinkled, rough or wavy leaves (PVY, PVX)
  • Dark dead streaks on leaf veins and stems; lower leaves die and hang down on the stem (severe PVY)
  • Plants are stunted and give fewer and smaller tubers
  • In some varieties, brown net-like strands inside the tuber (leafroll) or brown sunken rings on the tuber skin (some PVY strains)

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Potato leafroll virus (PLRV), Potato virus Y (PVY), Potato virus X (PVX) and other potato viruses.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Infected seed tubers are the main source; each one grows into a sick plant
  • Aphids carry the viruses from sick plants to healthy ones; leafroll needs a longer aphid feed, while PVY is passed within seconds
  • PVX and PVY also spread by contact: on cutting knives, tools and machines, and when leaves rub together
  • Volunteer potato plants, piles of thrown-away tubers and weeds near the field keep viruses and aphids alive
  • Replanting home-saved seed year after year builds up virus levels

What to Do

  • Walk the field soon after the plants come up, and then every week; mark plants with rolled, stiff or mottled leaves
  • Pull out the whole sick plant with its tubers early, before plants touch each other; carry it out of the field in a closed bag and bury or destroy it
  • Look for aphids under the leaves, and use yellow sticky traps or yellow water pans to see when winged aphids arrive
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether an aphid spray is needed, especially in a seed crop; sprays help less against PVY because aphids pass it so fast
  • Do not keep tubers from a field with many sick plants for use as seed

Don't

  • Don't use small tubers from virus-affected plants as seed
  • Don't cut seed tubers from different lots with the same knife without cleaning it

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the seed certification department if many plants in a field grown from certified seed show leafroll or mosaic, or if you want your seed crop tested for viruses.

Prevention

  • Plant certified seed potato from a reliable source, inspected by the seed certification department
  • Replace home-saved seed with fresh certified seed regularly, as your agriculture officer advises
  • If you grow your own seed, keep it in a separate plot, remove sick plants several times, and remove the tops (haulms) early to limit late aphid infection
  • Remove volunteer potatoes, piles of thrown-away tubers and weeds around the field
  • Clean and disinfect cutting knives and planting equipment
  • Plant at the recommended time so the crop avoids the peak of aphid flights where possible

Problems That Look Similar

  • Black scurf and stem canker

    Top leaves may roll and turn purplish, but stems have brown sunken cankers near the soil, small green tubers can form on the stem, and tubers carry black crusts that don't wash off.

  • Nutrient deficiency

    Yellowing or purple colour follows a pattern on old or young leaves across the whole field or soil patches, without stiff rolled leaves on scattered plants.

  • Herbicide (weedkiller) damage

    Twisted or cupped leaves on many plants at once, often along the field edge after a nearby weedkiller spray.
