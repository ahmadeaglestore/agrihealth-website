Mango Gall Midges (Leaf Gall and Flower Midge)

Many small wart-like bumps on mango leaves with tiny holes; flower clusters and young fruit dry and drop.

Overview

Gall midges are very small, fragile flies. Their maggots (larvae) feed inside mango tissue. The leaf gall midge lays eggs in tender new leaves; each larva makes a small wart-like swelling (gall) on the leaf. When the adult comes out, it leaves a tiny hole, mostly on the underside. Many galls make leaves curl, dry and fall early, and weaken young trees. Other midge species lay eggs in flower clusters (bour): their larvae tunnel inside the flower stalk so the flowers dry, and later attacks make young fruit turn yellow and drop.

In Pakistan, gall midges have been reported damaging mango in all mango-growing areas since about 2005. A study in Rahim Yar Khan found eggs, larvae and adults active from about January–February to April, with adults flying until May. Fully grown larvae drop from the tree to the soil under the canopy to pupate, so the soil under trees is an important place to attack them. Crowded commercial orchards were more damaged than single trees on farms.

Symptoms

  • Small reddish spots on tender new leaves where eggs were laid
  • Many small, raised, wart-like or pimple-like galls on the leaf blade, seen on both sides of the leaf
  • Tiny exit holes in the galls, mostly on the underside, sometimes with an empty pupal skin sticking out
  • Heavily galled leaves curl, dry and drop early; young trees grow poorly
  • Flower clusters with dark spots or holes on the stalk that dry, turn black and drop their flowers
  • Young fruits turn yellow and fall soon after fruit set

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Tiny gall-midge flies: Procontarinia matteiana (leaf gall midge), Procontarinia mangiferae and other mango midges on flowers and young fruit.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adults fly from tree to tree and lay eggs in tender new leaves and flower clusters
  • Larvae drop to the soil under the tree to pupate, and new adults come out from there
  • Several generations can follow each other while new leaves and flowers are available, mainly from late winter to spring
  • Moving infested nursery plants carries the pest to new areas
  • Dense, crowded orchards with soil left undisturbed under the trees suffer more

What to Do

  • Pick off and burn heavily galled leaves on young trees and nursery plants
  • Cut off and destroy flower clusters damaged by midge larvae
  • Rake or lightly hoe the soil under the canopy to expose and destroy larvae and pupae
  • Hang yellow sticky traps in the canopy from late winter to see when adult midges are flying
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a neem seed kernel extract or another approved spray is advised, and when to use it

Don't

  • Do not leave fallen galled leaves and dropped flower clusters under the trees

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the Mango Research Institute if many flower clusters are drying and dropping at flowering, or if most new leaves on young trees are covered with galls.

Prevention

  • Buy gall-free plants from a registered nursery and check the leaves before planting
  • Plough or rake the soil under trees after the season to expose resting larvae and pupae to sun
  • Collect and destroy damaged leaves, flower clusters and dropped fruit regularly
  • Prune to keep the canopy open and avoid overcrowded planting
  • Monitor with yellow sticky traps every season from January to May
  • Protect natural enemies (small parasitic wasps) by avoiding unnecessary sprays

Problems That Look Similar

  • Bacterial canker of mango

    Spots are black and angular with a yellow ring, later dry and crack, and also appear on fruit; there are no exit holes or larvae inside.

  • Mango hoppers

    Flowers dry and drop too, but small wedge-shaped insects jump on the flowers and leaves become sticky and black.

  • Powdery mildew of mango

    Flower clusters and young leaves are covered with white powder instead of galls or holes.
