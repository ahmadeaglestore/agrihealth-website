Downy Mildew of Cabbage and Cauliflower

Yellow angular patches on top of cabbage-family leaves, with grey-white downy growth underneath.

Overview

Downy mildew is caused by a fungus-like water mould that attacks cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli, radish, turnip, mustard and other crops of the cabbage family. It is most damaging on young seedlings in the nursery, where it can weaken or kill them, but it also spots the leaves of older plants and can blacken cauliflower curds and cabbage heads.

It is a disease of cool, damp weather (best about 8–16 °C), with dew or rain keeping the leaves wet, so the risk is highest in cool winter weather in the nursery and young crop. On mustard and canola it is often found together with white rust, and the two can make the flower stalks swollen and twisted.

Symptoms

  • On seedlings, white downy growth on the underside of the seed leaves, then yellowing and black speckling on top
  • On older leaves, small angular light-green to yellow patches on the upper side
  • Grey to white downy growth under the patches, best seen in the morning after a damp night
  • Patches turn brown and papery, sometimes with black specks
  • Cauliflower curds and cabbage heads can turn grey-brown to black, outside and inside
  • On mustard, swollen and twisted flower stalks, often together with white rust blisters

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Hyaloperonospora parasitica / H. brassicae (fungus-like water mould).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores form overnight and are carried by wind and rain splash the next morning
  • Cool, wet weather with long leaf wetness favours infection; spores can infect within a few hours on a wet leaf
  • Crowded, humid nursery beds with poor air movement
  • The water mould survives in old crop debris in the soil and on cabbage-family weeds and volunteer plants
  • Infected seedlings and seed can carry it to new fields
  • Shortage of potash (potassium) makes seedlings more easily infected

What to Do

  • Check the underside of seedling leaves early in the morning for grey-white downy growth
  • Remove and destroy badly infected seedlings and trays; do not transplant them
  • Water so that the leaves dry quickly; avoid watering in the early morning, and give one deep watering rather than many light ones
  • Thin crowded nursery beds to let air move between plants
  • If the disease is spreading in cool, damp weather, ask your agriculture officer which fungicide is approved for downy mildew and when to use it

Don't

  • Don't sprinkle water over the leaves late in the day when they will stay wet all night

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many nursery seedlings show downy growth and yellowing, or if cauliflower curds and cabbage heads start to blacken.

Prevention

  • Raise or buy healthy, disease-free seedlings
  • Use clean, good-quality seed from a reliable source
  • Choose a nursery site with good air flow, away from other cabbage-family crops and piles of crop waste
  • Keep balanced nutrition with enough potash
  • Remove cabbage-family weeds such as wild mustard
  • Plough in crop debris after harvest and rotate with crops outside the cabbage family for 2–3 years

Problems That Look Similar

  • Black rot

    V-shaped yellow areas from the leaf edge with blackened veins; no downy growth under the leaf.

  • Alternaria leaf spot

    Round brown to black spots with rings like a target; no grey-white downy growth underneath.

  • White rust

    Raised, shiny white blisters under the leaves that burst into chalky powder, instead of a soft downy layer.
