Rust of Sugarcane (Brown and Orange Rust)

Small, long brown or orange pustules on the leaves that leave a rusty powder on your fingers.

Overview

Rust is a fungal leaf disease of sugarcane. Two kinds are recorded in Pakistan: brown rust and orange rust. Both make small, long pustules on the leaves that burst and release a rusty powder of spores. Badly attacked leaves die early, so the plant makes less food and cane weight and sugar can fall.

Brown rust likes cool nights with dew and warm, sunny days, while orange rust likes warm, humid weather. Rust is one of the diseases that Punjab's pest warning teams check in their summer sugarcane field surveys. Many varieties resist rust, so the variety you grow matters most.

Symptoms

  • Tiny, long, pale yellow or light green spots on the leaves
  • The spots grow into long brown, reddish-brown or orange pustules, mostly on the underside of the leaf
  • The pustules burst and release a brown or orange powder that comes off on your fingers
  • Brown rust pustules are long and spread evenly, more towards the leaf tip; orange rust pustules are shorter, orange and in clusters, more towards the leaf base
  • Spots join into large dead patches and the leaves dry early
  • From a distance a badly attacked field looks reddish-brown or rusty

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Puccinia melanocephala (brown rust) and Puccinia kuehnii (orange rust), spread by wind-blown spores.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores are carried by wind and wind-blown rain, even over very long distances
  • Wet leaves or dew are needed for the spores to infect
  • Brown rust is favoured by cool, dewy nights and warm days; orange rust by warm, humid weather
  • Growing one susceptible variety over a large area
  • Fields that have recently received fresh press mud (mill mud) are reported to be very prone to rust

What to Do

  • Rub a few spotted leaves between your fingers: a rusty powder means rust
  • Note which variety is attacked and how many plants show pustules
  • In a light attack, strip off badly rusted lower leaves and burn them away from the field
  • If the attack is heavy on a weak variety, ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide spray is needed and which one is recommended
  • Plan to replace a badly attacked variety with a rust-resistant one

Don't

  • Do not spray fungicide without advice; most resistant varieties do not need any spray for rust

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if rust is spreading fast and many leaves are drying early, especially on a variety known to be weak against rust. They can confirm the type of rust and advise on spraying and a better variety.

Prevention

  • Grow a rust-resistant variety recommended by your agriculture department
  • Do not plant one variety on all your land; grow two or more varieties
  • Remove and burn badly rusted leaves and trash
  • Watch fields closely after applying fresh press mud
  • Check the crop regularly during cool, dewy spells and humid weather

Problems That Look Similar

  • Brown spot

    Oval reddish-brown spots with a yellow halo; no powder comes off when you rub the leaf.

  • Helminthosporium leaf spot

    Small dark red to brown spots that grow along the leaf; they are not raised pustules and leave no powder.

  • Pyrilla with sooty mould

    A black sooty coating that wipes off, with jumping hoppers and nymphs on the leaves; no pustules.
