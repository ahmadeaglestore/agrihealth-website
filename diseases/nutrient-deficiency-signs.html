Nutrient Deficiency Signs: Potassium, Magnesium, Iron, Boron and Others

Leaves show colour patterns: scorched edges, yellow between green veins, pale new leaves or deformed growing tips.

Overview

Plants need many nutrients, and when one is short the leaves often show a typical colour pattern. Nitrogen and zinc shortage have their own guides; this guide helps you read the other patterns. The golden rule is to see where the problem starts. Nutrients that can move inside the plant, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium, are moved from old leaves to new ones when they are short, so the older, lower leaves show signs first. Nutrients that cannot move, such as calcium, sulphur, boron, iron, manganese, zinc and copper, show signs first on the newest leaves, growing tips, flowers and fruit.

In Pakistan many soils are alkaline and calcareous (rich in lime) with little organic matter, so iron, zinc, manganese and boron are often locked up even when present; a NARC study found boron shortage in about half of the cotton area it surveyed. Salty soil, waterlogging, drought, cold, root rot and nematodes can also cause deficiency-like signs. Diseases, viruses, insects and herbicide drift can look similar too. A regular pattern seen on many plants across the field points to nutrition, while random patches point more to disease or pests. A soil and leaf test is the surest way to know which nutrient is short.

Symptoms

  • Potassium: older leaves turn yellow and then brown and dry along the edges and tips (scorch), while the centre stays green; on date palm, the tips of the oldest fronds turn orange and dry and break off
  • Magnesium: older leaves turn yellow between the veins while the veins stay green, sometimes with reddish tints; on palms, broad yellow bands along the edges of old fronds
  • Iron: the newest leaves turn pale yellow to almost white with green veins, while older leaves stay green; worst on lime-rich or waterlogged soil
  • Manganese: young leaves turn pale between the veins with a fine green net, sometimes with tan or grey specks
  • Boron: growing tips are deformed or die, new growth is short and bunched, leaves are thick and brittle, flowers drop, and fruit or stems may crack or become corky or hollow
  • Calcium: young leaves are distorted with burnt tips; in tomato and chilli, a dark sunken patch forms at the flower end of the fruit (blossom-end rot)
  • Sulphur: the whole plant is pale yellow-green, often the new leaves first, with thin stems and slow growth

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Shortage of potassium, magnesium, iron, manganese, boron, calcium, sulphur or other nutrients, often caused by soil pH, salts, water or root problems.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Alkaline, lime-rich soils with little organic matter lock up iron, zinc, manganese and boron
  • Sandy soils and heavy irrigation or rain wash out potassium, magnesium, sulphur and boron
  • Salty soil, waterlogging, drought, cold soil or damaged roots stop uptake even when the nutrient is present
  • Using only nitrogen and phosphorus fertilisers for years, without potash or organic manure
  • Too much of one nutrient can block another, for example too much potassium can cause magnesium shortage and too much phosphorus can reduce zinc and iron uptake
  • High-yielding crops and fruit trees with a heavy crop remove large amounts of nutrients

What to Do

  • Note which leaves show the signs first, the old lower leaves or the newest ones, and what the pattern looks like
  • Rule out other causes: look at the roots, check for waterlogging, look under leaves for pests, and think about recent herbicide sprays nearby
  • Compare with healthy plants or a healthy part of the field
  • Take soil and leaf samples to the agriculture department's soil and water testing lab
  • Give the missing nutrient as fertiliser or leaf spray only on the advice of your agriculture officer or the lab
  • Add compost or well-rotted farmyard manure to improve the soil

Don't

  • Don't apply many fertilisers or micronutrient mixtures blindly; too much of one nutrient can cause another shortage
  • Don't assume all yellowing is nitrogen shortage; more urea will not cure iron, magnesium or potassium shortage

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if the leaf pattern does not match any clear deficiency, if signs keep spreading after fertiliser, or if only scattered plants or patches are affected, which may mean disease, pests or herbicide damage.

Prevention

  • Test your soil every few years and fertilise according to the result
  • Use balanced fertiliser, including potash where the soil test shows it is needed
  • Add organic matter regularly: farmyard manure, compost, green manure and crop residues
  • Keep good drainage and avoid waterlogging; reclaim salty soils on the agriculture department's advice
  • Keep roots healthy by managing nematodes and root rots
  • Rotate crops, including legumes such as chickpea or berseem

Problems That Look Similar

  • Nitrogen deficiency

    The whole plant is evenly pale, older lower leaves yellow first from the tip along the midrib, with no green vein pattern.

  • Zinc deficiency

    Small, narrow or bunched new leaves, and pale or white bands on young leaves, often in patches of the field.

  • Blossom-end rot of tomato

    A dark, sunken, leathery patch at the flower end of tomato or chilli fruit, linked to calcium and uneven watering.
