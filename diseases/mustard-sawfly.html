Mustard Sawfly

Dark grey-black larvae eat holes in young mustard leaves from the edges; they curl up and drop when touched.

Overview

The mustard sawfly is a small wasp-like insect, not a true fly. The adult has a black head and chest, an orange body and smoky wings with black veins. The larvae do the damage. They are dark greenish-black, wrinkled 'false caterpillars' with eight pairs of fleshy legs under the belly, more than true caterpillars have. When disturbed, a larva drops to the ground, curls up and pretends to be dead. Larvae feed for about two weeks, then go into the soil and pupate in a small cocoon that looks like a grain of sand.

It attacks mustard, canola, toria, radish, turnip, cabbage and cauliflower. Most damage is at the seedling stage of the rabi crop: in studies from north India it appeared from October and did the most damage in October and November, mainly in the first two months after sowing. Mild weather favours it, while severe cold and very hot weather reduce it. Heavy attack can strip seedlings so the crop has to be re-sown, and feeding on older plants can stop them from setting seed.

Symptoms

  • Small, irregular nibbled patches and holes in young leaves
  • Leaves eaten from the edges inward towards the midrib, with many shot holes
  • Seedlings stripped of leaves until only the veins are left; they may dry and die
  • Dark greenish-black, wrinkled larvae on the leaves, often in groups on the cut edges, mostly in the morning and evening
  • Larvae curl up and drop to the ground when the plant is touched
  • Older plants with heavy feeding make fewer pods and seed

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Athalia lugens proxima (mustard sawfly); the larvae are leaf-eating 'false caterpillars'.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adults fly into fields from nearby cabbage-family crops and weeds
  • Larvae pupate in the soil, so untilled fields and field borders carry the pest over
  • Mild autumn weather favours larvae; severe cold and very hot weather reduce them
  • The young seedling stage is most at risk
  • Weedy fields with wild mustard-family weeds help numbers build up

What to Do

  • Check young plants often in the first month after sowing, in the morning and evening when larvae feed in the open
  • On small plots, hand-pick larvae or shake plants over a tray, and destroy them
  • Irrigate the seedling crop when larvae are present; many larvae fall to the ground and drown
  • Protect natural enemies, such as small parasitic wasps that attack the larvae, by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum sprays
  • If larvae are many and seedlings are being stripped, ask your agriculture officer whether an insecticide is needed and which one is recommended

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if larvae are stripping many seedlings in the first weeks after sowing, so you can decide quickly about control or re-sowing.

Prevention

  • Plough deeply in summer to expose pupae in the soil to heat and birds
  • Sow at the recommended time; early sowing is advised against this pest
  • Keep the field and its borders clean of cabbage-family weeds
  • Check cabbage and cauliflower nurseries regularly for larvae
  • Fill gaps quickly by re-sowing if seedlings are lost

Problems That Look Similar

  • Diamondback moth

    Small, pale green caterpillars that wriggle backwards fast and hang on silk threads, making small 'windows' in leaves; sawfly larvae are dark grey-black and drop and play dead.

  • Leaf-eating caterpillars

    True caterpillars, such as armyworms, have at most five pairs of fleshy legs under the belly; sawfly larvae have more.

  • Painted bug

    Seedlings wilt and dry, but leaves are not eaten; small black-and-orange bugs are seen on the plants.
