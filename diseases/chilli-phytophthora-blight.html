Phytophthora Blight & Root Rot (Chilli Wilt)

Chilli plants wilt suddenly and die, with a dark, soft rot on the stem at soil level.

Overview

Phytophthora blight is caused by Phytophthora capsici, a water mould that lives in soil. It rots the roots and stem base of chilli, so plants wilt and die quickly. It also rots the fruits of pumpkin, gourds, cucumber and melons, and can attack brinjal.

It needs free water. Soil that stays soaked for only a few hours in warm weather (about 24–33 °C) can start infection. That is why dead plants often appear first in low, wet parts of the field after heavy rain or heavy irrigation, especially in summer and the monsoon.

Symptoms

  • Plants wilt suddenly and die, often first in low spots where water stands
  • A dark green, water-soaked patch on the stem at soil level, which later turns brown and dry and circles the stem
  • Roots turn dark brown and soft, with few small roots left
  • Brown spots on leaves in wet weather
  • Fruits rot and are often covered by a white powdery or mouldy layer; fruits touching the soil are hit first
  • In pumpkin and squash the vine base rots; in cucumber and melons mainly the fruits rot

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Phytophthora capsici (soil- and water-borne water mould).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Swimming spores move in rain and irrigation water from sick plants to healthy ones
  • Water splash carries spores onto stems, leaves and fruits
  • Thick-walled spores survive in soil for many years
  • Infected seedlings, and soil carried on tools, shoes and machines
  • Heavy clay or hard-packed soil, poor drainage, and long or very frequent irrigation

What to Do

  • Pull out wilted plants with their roots and the soil around them, bag them and destroy them away from the field
  • Stop over-watering and let the field drain
  • Don't let irrigation water flow from the sick patch to healthy rows
  • Wash tools and shoes after working in the sick patch
  • Ask your agriculture officer to confirm the cause and advise whether any approved treatment is useful

Don't

  • Don't throw rotten plants or fruits in the field or in water channels
  • Don't give heavy flood irrigation while plants are wilting

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if plants are dying in patches after rain or irrigation, so the cause can be confirmed; wilting can also come from other fungi, bacteria or nematodes.

Prevention

  • Grow chilli on raised beds or ridges in well-drained soil
  • Avoid low-lying fields and fields where the disease has occurred before
  • Avoid long or very frequent irrigation; drip or careful furrow watering is better than flooding
  • Use healthy transplants from a clean nursery
  • Rotate for at least two years with crops that are not hosts, such as cereals
  • Use clean water and wash equipment before moving from an infected field to a clean one

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fusarium wilt

    Plants yellow and wilt more slowly and the inside of the stem has brown streaks, but there is no dark, soft rot at the stem base.

  • Root-knot nematode

    Plants are stunted and droop in the hot hours, and the roots have knots or swellings.

  • Damping-off in the nursery

    Young seedlings fall over at soil level in the nursery bed, usually where the bed is over-watered or poorly drained.
