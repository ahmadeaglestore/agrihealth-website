Grassy Shoot Disease of Sugarcane

A clump with many thin, grass-like white or yellow shoots; canes stay short and thin.

Overview

Grassy shoot is a disease of sugarcane caused by a phytoplasma, a very small bacteria-like organism that lives in the plant's sap tubes. Affected clumps produce many thin, white or yellow, grass-like shoots and give few useful canes.

It usually shows in young crops a few months after planting and occurs in scattered clumps rather than over the whole field. It is carried mainly in setts cut from diseased canes and is also spread by leafhoppers. Keeping an infected crop as ratoon carries the disease into the next crop.

Symptoms

  • Thin, papery, white young leaves at the top of the shoot
  • Many thin white or yellow tillers from the base, giving a bushy, grass-like clump
  • Stunted canes with short internodes
  • Side buds on the cane sprout early
  • Affected clumps are scattered here and there in the field
  • Usually first seen when the crop is about 3–4 months old

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Sugarcane grassy shoot phytoplasma ('Candidatus Phytoplasma sacchari'), carried in setts and by leafhoppers.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Planting setts cut from diseased canes, the main way it reaches new fields
  • Sap-sucking leafhoppers carry it from diseased to healthy plants; the leafhopper Deltocephalus vulgaris has been found to be a vector
  • Ratooning an infected crop carries the disease into the next crop

What to Do

  • Mark and count the clumps with grassy shoots
  • Dig out diseased clumps with their roots and burn them
  • In a young crop, fill the gaps with healthy plants
  • Do not take seed cane from this field
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether to keep the ratoon and whether leafhopper control is needed

Don't

  • Do not ratoon a crop with many grassy-shoot clumps

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if grassy clumps are appearing in many places, especially in a seed-cane field. They can confirm the disease and advise whether the crop is fit for ratoon or seed.

Prevention

  • Plant healthy setts from a disease-free field or seed-cane nursery
  • Use heat-treated (steam or hot-air) seed cane where a seed-cane centre provides it
  • Rogue out diseased plants from seed nurseries
  • Grow a variety recommended for your area
  • Check young crops regularly and remove diseased clumps early

Problems That Look Similar

  • Whip smut

    Thin grassy shoots too, but a long black whip with black powder comes out of the top.

  • Ratoon stunting disease

    Canes are short but the leaves stay normal green and not grassy; tiny orange dots are seen at the nodes when the cane is split.

  • Top borer bunchy top

    Side shoots grow at the top of a cane with a dead heart and a caterpillar tunnel, not many thin white shoots from the base.
