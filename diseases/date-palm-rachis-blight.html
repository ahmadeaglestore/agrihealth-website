Rachis Blight (Leaf Base Blight) of Date Palm

Brown streaks along the date palm leaf midrib; leaflets on one side die while the other side stays green.

Overview

Rachis blight, also called petiole blight or leaf base blight, is a fungal disease of the leaf stalk and the long midrib (rachis) of palm fronds. Brown or reddish-brown long streaks or patches form on the rachis or leaf stalk. The fungus destroys the water-carrying tissue, so the leaflets joined to the damaged part die, often on one side of the leaf only, while the other side stays green. It starts on the oldest living fronds and slowly moves up the crown. Several fungi can cause it with almost the same signs; on date palm, Diplodia disease makes yellowish-brown streaks along the underside of the leaf base and rachis while the upper leaf may stay green, and it can also kill young offshoots. Rachis blight seldom kills a mature palm.

The fungi mostly enter through wounds from pruning and offshoot removal, and spores spread from dead fronds; high humidity is thought to favour infection. The main worry is that rachis blight looks very much like serious wilt diseases, such as Fusarium wilt and the sudden decline of date palms that has caused heavy losses in Khairpur and other date areas. Only a lab test can tell them apart. If many fronds are dying fast, younger leaves are affected, or several palms show it, get samples tested through your agriculture officer.

Symptoms

  • A long brown or reddish-brown streak along the rachis or leaf stalk, often on the underside and starting near the leaf base
  • Leaflets on the side of the streak turn yellow, then brown and die, while the other side stays green
  • It starts on the oldest living fronds and moves to younger fronds over months
  • When the rachis is cut across with a saw, the inside is brown on the affected side
  • Offshoots turn yellow and die, before or after planting (Diplodia)
  • Small dark fungal bodies may be seen on the dead parts of the rachis

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Several wound-entering fungi; Diplodia (Lasiodiplodia), Phomopsis, Phoma and others are reported on palms, and the exact cause needs a lab test.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The fungi enter through pruning cuts and wounds from removing offshoots
  • Spores spread by wind and rain from dead fronds left on the palm or on the ground
  • Unclean pruning tools carry the fungi from palm to palm
  • High humidity, overhead watering and crowded nurseries favour infection

What to Do

  • Check many fronds: if only a few old fronds show one-sided death, cut them out
  • Remove and burn badly affected fronds, but leave enough green fronds, especially if the palm is also short of nutrients
  • Clean and disinfect pruning tools between palms, and use a saw for clean cuts
  • Protect fresh cuts on offshoots and palms as advised by your agriculture department
  • Send a piece of affected rachis to a lab through your agriculture officer, especially if many palms or younger fronds are affected

Don't

  • Don't remove many green fronds at once; heavy pruning weakens the palm and makes more wounds

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer quickly if fronds are dying fast, younger fronds are affected, or several palms show one-sided leaf death, because serious wilt or sudden decline diseases look similar.

Prevention

  • Plant healthy offshoots from trusted sources
  • Prune only dead or dying fronds, about once a year
  • Disinfect tools before moving to the next palm
  • Water at the base, not over the crown
  • Burn dead and diseased fronds instead of leaving them in the orchard
  • Keep palms well fed and well drained

Problems That Look Similar

  • Sudden decline of date palm

    Many fronds dry from the outside inwards over weeks to months and the whole palm can die; a lab test is needed.

  • Black scorch

    Hard, black, burnt-looking patches on leaf bases, fruit stalks and the growing point, sometimes with a bent crown.

  • Potassium deficiency

    Tips and edges of the oldest fronds turn orange-yellow and dry on both sides of the rachis, with no brown streak along it.
