Leaf Spots and Phomopsis Blight of Brinjal

Brown spots on brinjal leaves, some with rings; sunken dark spots and rot on fruit; seedlings may collapse.

Overview

Two common fungal diseases cause brown spots on brinjal leaves. Phomopsis blight, caused by Phomopsis vexans, is one of the most damaging brinjal diseases in South Asia. It can attack seedlings, leaves, stems and fruit. On leaves it makes grey to brown spots; on fruit it makes pale, sunken spots that turn into a soft rot, and the fruit can end up dry, black and shrivelled. This fungus was recorded on brinjal in Pakistan in the early 1980s. Alternaria leaf spot makes brown spots with rings, like a target, on the leaves and can also spot the fruit.

Both diseases like warm, humid or rainy weather, so they are usually worst during and after the monsoon and in crops with thick leaf cover. Phomopsis is carried in seed and survives in infected plant remains in the soil, so a diseased nursery can spread it to the whole field. Grey leaf spots caused by Cercospora have their own guide.

Symptoms

  • In the nursery, seedlings rot at the stem near soil level, fall over and die (Phomopsis)
  • Round or irregular grey to brown spots on leaves; the centre of Phomopsis spots often turns light grey with tiny black dots
  • Brown spots with clear rings like a target, mostly on older leaves (Alternaria)
  • Spots join together, and leaves dry and drop early
  • Dark brown sunken patches (cankers) on stems and branches; a ringed stem can make the branch or whole plant dry up
  • Pale, sunken spots on fruit that grow fast and become soft and rotten; later the fruit turns black, dry and shrivelled, covered with tiny black dots
  • Large deep spots on fruit with Alternaria; such fruit turns yellow and drops early

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Phomopsis vexans (Phomopsis blight and fruit rot) and Alternaria species such as A. melongenae and A. solani (leaf spot with rings).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Phomopsis is carried in and on seed taken from infected fruit, so home-saved seed can bring it into the nursery
  • Both fungi survive on infected leaves, stems and fruit left in the field
  • Rain splash, irrigation water, tools and insects carry spores from plant to plant
  • Hot, humid, rainy weather and long periods of wet leaves favour infection
  • Dense planting, weeds and overhead watering keep leaves wet for longer; nightshade-family weeds can also carry Phomopsis

What to Do

  • Pick off spotted leaves and rotten fruit and take them out of the field; bury them deep or destroy them, don't leave them on the ground
  • Pull out and destroy collapsed nursery seedlings and plants with ringed, cankered stems
  • Keep the leaves as dry as possible: water at the base of the plants and avoid watering late in the day
  • Improve air flow by removing weeds and some crowded lower leaves
  • If spots keep spreading in wet weather, ask your agriculture officer for a fungicide approved for brinjal, and keep the waiting period before picking

Don't

  • Don't save seed from spotted or rotten fruit
  • Don't throw rotten fruit at the field edge or into irrigation channels

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many nursery seedlings are collapsing, if fruit rot is spreading through the field in wet weather, or if you are not sure whether the spots are fungal or bacterial.

Prevention

  • Use healthy seed from a reliable source; ask your agriculture officer about seed treatment (hot water or an approved fungicide) against Phomopsis
  • Raise the nursery on clean soil that has not grown brinjal, tomato, chilli or potato recently
  • Rotate brinjal with crops outside the tomato family for three to four years where possible
  • Grow varieties with better tolerance where your agriculture department recommends them
  • Leave enough space between plants and rows so leaves dry quickly
  • Clear crop remains and nightshade-type weeds after the last picking

Problems That Look Similar

  • Cercospora leaf spot

    Angular to round spots with pale grey centres and yellow edges on the leaves; it does not rot the fruit or make stem cankers.

  • Phytophthora fruit rot

    Water-soaked spots on fruit that spread fast in the rains, often with white cottony growth on the fruit; leaves are not spotted.

  • Brinjal shoot and fruit borer

    Drooping, withered shoot tips and fruit with small round holes and droppings inside; you find a pinkish caterpillar, not spots.
