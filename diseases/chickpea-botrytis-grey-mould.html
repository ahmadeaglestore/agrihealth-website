Botrytis Grey Mould of Chickpea

Grey, fuzzy mould on chickpea flowers, pods and stems in dense, humid crops; flowers rot and drop, few pods set.

Overview

Botrytis grey mould is a fungal disease of chickpea caused by Botrytis cinerea. Flowers rot into a soft mass and drop, and stems, branches, leaves and pods get soft, water-soaked patches that are soon covered with a grey, fuzzy, dusty mould. Stem patches can girdle the stem so that branches break off, leaves fall and plants die in patches. Often the first thing farmers notice is that the crop flowers but sets very few pods, and infected pods have small, shrivelled seeds or none.

The disease needs long spells of cool, cloudy and humid weather, and it builds up inside dense, lush crops where the canopy stays damp, especially at flowering and podding. It is a major problem of chickpea in South Asia, and Pakistan has been part of regional research on it with India, Nepal and Bangladesh. In Pakistan it is mainly seen in the wetter north and in irrigated fields with heavy, closed growth, after winter or early spring rain. The fungus survives on infected seed and crop debris, so clean seed and an open crop are the best protection.

Symptoms

  • Crop flowers well but sets few pods
  • Flowers rot into a soft, grey mass and drop; leaves also drop
  • Soft, water-soaked, brown patches on stems, branches and leaf stalks, covered with grey, fuzzy, dusty mould in damp weather
  • Patches girdle the stem; tender branches break off at the rotten spot
  • Water-soaked, irregular patches on pods; pods hold small, shrivelled or no seeds, and seed may be discoloured
  • Plants die in patches inside dense parts of the crop; small black hard bodies may form on dead tissue

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Botrytis cinerea (grey mould fungus).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The fungus survives on infected seed, on crop debris and as small black resting bodies in soil
  • Spores are carried by air currents from plant to plant within the crop
  • Long spells of cool, cloudy, humid weather with rain or fog at flowering favour heavy attack
  • Dense, lush crops from high seed rate, heavy irrigation, excess growth or early sowing keep the canopy humid; lodged crops are worst
  • Old chickpea, lentil and other pulse stubble nearby can carry the fungus

What to Do

  • Check inside the crop, near the ground and in the densest parts, for grey fuzzy mould on flowers, stems and pods during cloudy, humid weather
  • Stop or delay irrigation while the weather is humid; more water makes the canopy wetter
  • Contact your agriculture officer quickly; a fungicide recommended by the agriculture department works best when used at the first signs, before the disease spreads through the crop
  • Keep seed for next season only from healthy fields, never from a badly infected crop
  • After harvest, collect and destroy or deeply bury infected crop remains

Don't

  • Don't give extra irrigation or nitrogen to a lush crop in cool, humid weather
  • Don't use seed from a field that had grey mould

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer at once if you see grey fuzzy mould on flowers or stems during cool, cloudy, humid weather, because the disease can spread through a dense crop very quickly.

Prevention

  • Use clean, healthy seed and treat it with an approved fungicide recommended by your agriculture department
  • Avoid a very dense crop: use the recommended seed rate and wider row spacing so air can move through the canopy
  • Sow at the recommended time for your area; very early sowing makes heavy growth that closes the canopy early
  • Avoid over-irrigation and excess fertiliser that cause too much leafy growth; grow compact, upright varieties where available
  • Plough deeply in summer and keep at least a three-year gap between chickpea or other pulse crops in the same field
  • Intercropping chickpea with linseed has been recommended to open the crop

Problems That Look Similar

  • Ascochyta blight (chickpea blight)

    Brown spots with dark dots in rings on leaves, stems and pods, and stems break; no grey fuzzy mould on rotting flowers.

  • White mold (Sclerotinia rot)

    White, cotton-like mould at the stem base or on branches, with large black hard bodies, not grey dusty mould.
