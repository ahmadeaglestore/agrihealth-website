Chilli Anthracnose (Fruit Rot) & Die-back

Sunken dark spots with rings on ripe chillies, and branches drying from the tip downward.

Overview

Anthracnose is a fungal disease of chilli caused by Colletotrichum species. It attacks twigs, causing die-back, and fruits, causing fruit rot. Ripe red fruits are hit hardest, and infected fruits lose colour, pungency and market value.

The disease is worst when rain or high humidity comes while fruits are ripening, for example during the monsoon. The fungus is carried on seed and in crop debris, so clean seed and field hygiene are the base of control.

Symptoms

  • Small, round, sunken spots on ripe fruit, often with dark edges
  • Spots grow larger and show rings of tiny black dots; in wet weather they get a pink or orange spore layer
  • Badly affected fruits turn straw-coloured or whitish, shrivel and drop early
  • Seeds inside rotten fruits turn rusty and get covered with fungus
  • Twig tips turn brown and dry, and the drying moves downward from the tip (die-back)
  • Small grey-brown spots with dark edges on leaves and stems

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Colletotrichum species (e.g. C. capsici / C. truncatum), a seed-borne fungus.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Infected seed is the main way the disease starts
  • Rain splash and wind-driven rain carry spores from sick fruits and twigs to healthy plants
  • Flies and other insects can carry spores from fruit to fruit
  • The fungus survives in crop debris and soil
  • Warm, humid weather (around 27 °C) with rain at ripening, and watering over the plants
  • Growing chilli, tomato, brinjal or potato on the same land year after year

What to Do

  • Pick off rotten fruits and cut out dried twigs a little below the dead part; burn or bury them away from the field
  • Harvest ripe fruits on time; don't leave them hanging on the plants
  • Avoid watering over the plants, and avoid field work while plants are wet
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide spray is needed and which product is approved

Don't

  • Don't keep seed from spotted or rotten fruits
  • Don't leave infected fruits and branches in the field after harvest

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture office if fruit rot is spreading quickly in rainy or humid weather, or if many branches are drying from the tip.

Prevention

  • Use clean, certified seed from healthy fruits; ask about seed treatment with an approved fungicide
  • Rotate with unrelated crops (not tomato, brinjal or potato) for two to three years
  • Make sure the field drains well and water does not stand
  • Control weeds and remove self-grown chilli plants
  • Plough deep or remove crop debris after harvest
  • Grow a variety recommended as tolerant for your area

Problems That Look Similar

  • Phytophthora blight

    The whole plant wilts quickly and the stem base turns dark and soft; rotting fruits have a white mouldy layer, not pink spore spots with rings.

  • Sunscald

    Pale, papery patches on the sun-facing side of the fruit, with no dark rings or spore layer.
