Sheath Blight of Rice

Oval grey-green patches on the stem near the water line that grow into a 'snake-skin' pattern.

Overview

Sheath blight is a fungal disease of rice. It starts on the leaf sheath (the part of the leaf wrapped around the stem) at or just above the water line, then climbs up to the upper leaves. The fungus survives in the soil as small, hard, brown bodies (sclerotia) and in old crop remains.

Scientists at NIAB Faisalabad report that sheath blight is now seen in the major rice-growing areas of Pakistan, with higher severity on the varieties farmers grow. It is a kharif problem that builds up in hot, very humid weather in thick, well-fertilised crops, mostly from late tillering to heading. Badly affected plants fill fewer grains and can fall over (lodge).

Symptoms

  • Oval or irregular, water-soaked, grey-green spots on the leaf sheath at or just above the water line
  • Spots grow and get greyish-white centres with brown or purplish edges
  • Spots join into large blotches with a banded 'snake-skin' look that climb up to the upper leaves and flag leaf
  • White, web-like fungal threads on sheaths in humid weather, and later small round brown sclerotia on the lesions
  • Damage shows in round patches in the field, starting where the crop is thickest
  • Weak stems that lodge, and poorly filled grain, mostly in the lower part of the panicle

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Rhizoctonia solani (soil-borne fungus).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Sclerotia and infected crop remains survive in the soil from one season to the next
  • Sclerotia float on irrigation water, stick to stems at the water line and start new infections
  • The fungus grows from tiller to tiller and plant to plant where leaves touch
  • Hot (about 28–32 °C) and very humid weather
  • Dense planting, a high seed rate and too much nitrogen fertiliser
  • Grassy weeds and some other crops such as maize, sorghum and sugarcane can also carry the fungus

What to Do

  • From late tillering, walk into the field, part the plants and check the lower stems near the water line
  • Mark the patches where you find it and check every few days how fast it climbs up the plant
  • Do not add more nitrogen fertiliser while the disease is spreading
  • Stop water from an infected field flowing into healthy fields
  • If the disease is climbing towards the upper leaves before or at heading, ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide is needed and which one is recommended

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if lesions reach the upper leaves or flag leaf before or at heading, or if many patches appear across the field.

Prevention

  • Use the recommended seed rate and plant spacing; avoid very thick nurseries and fields
  • Use balanced fertiliser and split nitrogen into smaller doses over the season; do not overuse urea
  • Remove grassy weeds from fields and bunds
  • Plough deeply after harvest to bury infected stubble and sclerotia
  • Add farmyard manure or green manure to build healthy soil
  • Ask your agriculture department which varieties are less affected in your area

Problems That Look Similar

  • Stem rot

    Black spots and dark rot on the outer sheath at the water line; inside the stem there are tiny black sclerotia and the stem breaks; no large grey-white 'snake-skin' blotches.

  • Sheath rot

    The rot is on the flag-leaf sheath around the young panicle, which does not come out properly; the lower sheaths are clean.

  • Rice blast

    Diamond-shaped spots on the leaf blades, not large banded blotches that start at the water line.
