Melon Fruit Fly

Young cucurbit fruits get tiny sting marks, turn yellow, rot and drop; small white maggots are inside.

Overview

The melon fruit fly is a serious pest of bitter gourd, cucumber, melons, pumpkin, sponge gourd and other cucurbits. The adult is an orange-brown fly, a little larger than a house fly, with yellow stripes on its back and smoky marks on the wing tips. The female pushes her eggs under the skin of young fruits and flowers.

White maggots feed inside the fruit, and bacteria carried by the fly make the flesh rot. Rot can be well advanced inside before it shows outside. Damage is usually worse in melons and during the monsoon. Because maggots drop to the soil to pupate, fallen fruits and the soil under the crop keep the pest going.

Symptoms

  • Small puncture marks (stings) on young fruits, sometimes with brown gummy liquid
  • The area around the sting turns soft and discoloured
  • Fruits turn yellow or ripen too early and start to rot
  • Young fruits drop from the vine
  • Cut fruits show soft, rotten flesh with small white maggots

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Zeugodacus cucurbitae (formerly Bactrocera cucurbitae), a fruit fly whose maggots feed inside fruit.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adult flies are strong fliers and move between fields and gardens
  • Infested fruits left on the vine or on the ground produce new flies
  • Maggots pupate in the soil under the crop
  • Warm, humid weather and the monsoon season increase numbers
  • Growing cucurbits continuously in the same area keeps the population high

What to Do

  • Collect all stung, rotting and fallen fruits at least twice a week
  • Destroy them: bury them deep, or seal them in a plastic bag and leave it in the sun for a day or more
  • Hang cue-lure traps for male melon flies in and around the field
  • In small plots, cover young fruits of bitter gourd, gourds and melons with paper or fine-mesh bags that do not touch the fruit
  • If fruit losses stay high, ask your agriculture officer about approved bait sprays or other control

Don't

  • Don't leave infested or fallen fruits in the field or put them in compost

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if many fruits are being stung despite field cleaning and traps, or if you need advice on approved bait sprays.

Prevention

  • Keep the field clean of damaged and fallen fruits through the whole season
  • Rake or plough the soil after harvest, and plough deeply in summer before sowing, to expose pupae to sun and birds
  • Rotate with non-host crops such as leafy vegetables or maize
  • Grow early-maturing varieties and adjust sowing dates to avoid the peak fly period
  • Use traps and fruit bags from flowering onwards
  • Work with neighbours, because flies move between fields

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fruit flies of fruit trees

    Related flies that mainly attack mango, guava and citrus; the damage looks similar but is on tree fruits.

  • Fruit rot (fungal or bacterial)

    Rot starts at the flower end or in wet patches, but there are no sting marks and no maggots inside.

  • Red pumpkin beetle

    Orange-red beetles that make holes in leaves and flowers; fruits are not stung and have no maggots inside.
