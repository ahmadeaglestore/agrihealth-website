Bacterial Wilt of Tomato, Potato, Brinjal and Chilli

Plants suddenly wilt while the leaves are still green; a cut stem in water gives off milky white threads.

Overview

Bacterial wilt is caused by a soil bacterium that gets into the roots and blocks the water pipes inside the stem. Plants wilt suddenly, often while the leaves are still green, and then die. It attacks tomato, potato, brinjal, chilli and many other plants. In potato the same disease is called brown rot because it rots the tubers from the inside.

It is a disease of warm to hot weather (about 25–35 °C) and moist soil. EPPO lists it as widespread in Pakistan; it has been found in wilting chilli fields across the country and in potatoes in northern valleys. No spray cures it, and once the bacteria are in a field they stay for a long time, so prevention and quick removal of sick plants are the only real control.

Symptoms

  • The youngest leaves droop first, while the plant is still green
  • At first plants wilt in the hot afternoon and seem to recover at night, then they wilt for good within a few days
  • Inside the lower stem the water pipes turn brown; later the stem base may rot
  • Stem-in-water test: cut a finger-length piece of the lower stem, hang it in a clear glass of water, and milky white threads stream out of the cut end
  • Potato: plants wilt and yellow; cut tubers show a brown ring, a sticky pale ooze comes from the ring or the eyes, and soil sticks to the eyes

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Ralstonia solanacearum species complex (soil- and water-borne bacterium); called brown rot in potato.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The bacteria live in soil and in infected plant remains, and enter through root wounds made by nematodes, insects, hoeing or transplanting
  • Irrigation water that has passed through an infected field, and soil on tools, shoes and machinery, carry the bacteria to new places
  • Infected seed potatoes and nursery plants bring it to new fields; seed tubers can carry it without showing any sign
  • Warm to hot weather (about 25–35 °C) and wet soil make it worse
  • Volunteer potatoes and some weeds can carry it from one season to the next

What to Do

  • Do the stem-in-water test on a freshly wilted plant to confirm the cause
  • Pull out wilted plants with their roots and the soil around them, put them in a bag, and destroy them away from the field
  • Stop water from running from the affected patch into healthy parts; water the healthy part first or block the channel
  • Clean tools, shoes and implements after working in the affected patch
  • Ask your agriculture officer to confirm the problem and advise on the next crop; no chemical spray or soil drench cures bacterial wilt

Don't

  • Don't spend money on sprays for this wilt; none of them cures it
  • Don't take seed potatoes, nursery plants or soil from an affected field to other fields

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if the stem-in-water test shows milky threads, if many plants wilt in hot weather, or if potato tubers show a brown ring with ooze. If you grow seed potatoes, also tell your seed certification staff.

Prevention

  • Use certified, disease-free seed potatoes and healthy nursery plants raised in clean soil
  • Rotate for several years with non-host crops such as wheat, maize, beans or cabbage, not tomato, potato, brinjal or chilli
  • In fields that have had the disease, use resistant varieties, or tomato plants grafted onto resistant rootstocks
  • Avoid wounding roots when hoeing, and control root-knot nematodes
  • Do not use irrigation water that has passed through infected fields, and improve drainage
  • Remove volunteer potatoes and tomato-family weeds

Problems That Look Similar

  • Fusarium or Verticillium wilt

    Slower; lower leaves turn yellow first, often on one side of the plant, and no milky threads come out in the water test.

  • Root-knot nematode

    Plants wilt in the heat and stay small, and the roots have knots or swellings.

  • Phytophthora blight of chilli

    Dark, water-soaked rot at the stem base, often after heavy watering; no milky ooze from the cut stem.
