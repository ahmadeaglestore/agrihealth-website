Graphiola Leaf Spot (False Smut) of Date Palm

Tiny yellow-brown spots with raised black cups on older date palm leaflets; yellow powder and threads come out.

Overview

Graphiola leaf spot, also called false smut, is a fungal disease of date palms caused by Graphiola phoenicis. It makes tiny yellow, brown or black spots on both sides of the leaflets, and also on the leaf midrib (rachis) and leaf base. From these spots the fungus breaks through the leaf surface and forms small, raised, black, cup-shaped bodies (sori). Yellow powdery spores and short pale threads come out of the mature cups. Spots are most common on the older fronds. The fungus has a very long hidden period: spots appear about ten to eleven months after infection, so what you see now started almost a year ago.

The disease is worst in humid areas and is mostly absent from dry oases. Heavy infection makes leaflets yellow and die early, so fronds live for a shorter time and yield can fall; in light attack it is mostly a cosmetic problem. In a trial at Abohar in nearby Indian Punjab, Zahidi was resistant, Medjool was highly susceptible, and Deglet Noor, Shamran, Hayani and Khadrawi were susceptible. How common it is in Pakistan's date-growing areas is not well documented, so confirm it with your agriculture officer. Potassium shortage can also cause spots on old fronds, and removing many leaves from a palm that is short of nutrients makes it weaker.

Symptoms

  • Tiny yellow, brown or black spots on both sides of the leaflets, mostly on the oldest fronds
  • Small, raised, black, cup-like bodies break through the leaf surface in the spots
  • Yellow powdery spores and short pale threads come out of the black cups
  • Spots also on the leaf midrib (rachis) and leaf base
  • Heavily spotted leaflets turn yellow and dry early, and older fronds die sooner

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Graphiola phoenicis (a smut-like fungus).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores are carried by wind from infected fronds
  • Humid weather, dew and long leaf wetness favour infection
  • Spots appear almost a year after infection, so the disease builds up unseen
  • Crowded palms, poor air flow and watering that wets the leaves
  • Susceptible varieties are attacked more

What to Do

  • Look closely at the spots, if possible with a hand lens, for raised black cups; this separates Graphiola from potassium shortage and other leaf spots
  • If the palm is otherwise healthy, cut out and burn the most heavily spotted old fronds, but leave enough green fronds
  • Water at the base early in the morning so leaves do not stay wet
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide is needed; sprays only protect new leaves and do not clear existing spots

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if spots cover many fronds and leaves are dying early, or if you are not sure whether the spots are Graphiola, a nutrient shortage or another disease.

Prevention

  • Keep good spacing between palms for air flow
  • Prune and burn old infected fronds every year
  • Choose less susceptible varieties for new planting on your agriculture officer's advice
  • Keep palms well fed, especially with potassium, based on a soil test
  • Plant healthy offshoots

Problems That Look Similar

  • Potassium deficiency

    Tips of the oldest fronds turn orange-yellow and dry, with fine translucent or brown spots but no raised black cups.

  • White scale (Parlatoria)

    Small white or grey scale insects that can be scraped off with a fingernail; no black cups or yellow powder.

  • Black scorch

    Hard, black, burnt-looking patches on leaf bases, leaf stalks and the growing point, not tiny spots on leaflets.
