Tomato Mosaic Virus

Light and dark green mosaic on leaves, puckered or fern-like narrow leaves, and stunted tomato plants.

Overview

Tomato mosaic is a virus disease. Leaves show a patchy mix of light and dark green (mosaic), and become puckered, small or narrow, while plants stay short. Fewer fruits set, and fruit may get brown patches on the skin or brown streaks inside the wall. It is closely related to tobacco mosaic virus, which can also infect tomato and chilli.

In Pakistan it is common in tomato in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: NIFA Peshawar reports it in both winter and summer crops, and a study in Malakand found it in nurseries as well as fields. Unlike leaf curl, it is not spread by whitefly. It spreads by touch, on hands, tools and clothes during transplanting, pruning and picking, and through infected seed. No spray can cure an infected plant.

Symptoms

  • Light green and dark green patches (mosaic or mottling) on the leaves
  • Young leaves puckered, twisted and smaller than normal
  • In cool weather with little sunlight, leaves can become very narrow, like fern leaves or strings
  • Plants stunted and pale, with fewer fruits; young leaves may droop on sunny days
  • Fruit with brown patches on the skin or brown streaks inside the wall, and uneven ripening

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) and the closely related tobacco mosaic virus (TMV); spread by touch and seed, not by insects.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Sap carried on hands, tools and clothes during transplanting, tying, pruning and picking
  • Infected seed (mostly on the seed coat) and infected nursery plants
  • Cigarettes and other tobacco products can carry the virus to plants
  • The virus survives for a long time in plant remains in the soil (up to about two years in dry soil) and on tunnel structures and stakes
  • Infected weeds, chilli and potato plants near the field can be sources; insects are not the main way it spreads

What to Do

  • Pull out plants with mosaic as soon as you see them, put them in a bag and destroy them away from the field
  • Work in healthy parts of the crop first and in the affected area last each day
  • Wash hands with soap and water before and after handling plants, and clean knives and tools often
  • Do not smoke or use tobacco while working with tomato or chilli plants, and wash hands after using tobacco
  • Ask your agriculture officer to confirm, because mosaic can also be caused by other viruses spread by aphids or whitefly

Don't

  • Don't spray insecticides or fungicides hoping to cure it; no spray cures a virus
  • Don't save seed from infected plants

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if several plants in a nursery or field show mosaic, so the virus can be identified and the seed source checked.

Prevention

  • Buy certified seed of a variety resistant to ToMV/TMV where available
  • Save seed only from healthy plants
  • Raise the nursery in clean or sterilised soil and remove any seedling with mosaic
  • On fields that had the disease, rotate for at least two years away from tomato, chilli, brinjal, tobacco and cucurbits
  • Remove all crop remains, especially roots, after harvest, and keep seedbeds and field edges free of weeds
  • Clean tunnel structures, stakes and tools between crops

Problems That Look Similar

  • Tomato leaf curl virus

    Leaves curl upward and become small with yellow edges, plants look bushy, and whiteflies are present.

  • Hormone weedkiller damage

    Twisted, narrow leaves appear on many kinds of plants at once after spraying nearby, not only on tomato.

  • Cucumber mosaic virus

    Can give very thin, shoestring-like leaves; it is spread by aphids, often from nearby cucurbits and weeds.
