Powdery Mildew of Citrus

White to grey powder on young citrus leaves and green twigs; leaves twist and pale, and young fruit may drop.

Overview

Powdery mildew of citrus is a fungal disease that makes a whitish, powdery coating on young leaves and green twigs, mostly on the upper side of the leaves. Young leaves turn pale greyish-green and become twisted. In bad attacks, leaves fall, young shoots wither and twigs die back, and flowers and young fruit can also be coated and drop early. All citrus types can be affected; in India, mandarins, sweet oranges and tangerines are the most susceptible.

The spores are carried by wind and survive on fallen leaves; they also move on hands, clothes, tools and vehicles, and with infected nursery plants. Like other powdery mildews, it attacks soft new growth, so it is most likely on new flush in spring, in crowded orchards and in shady nursery beds with poor air flow. The disease is recorded in India and other parts of Asia, but how common it is in Pakistani citrus orchards is not well documented, so confirm it with your agriculture officer. Do not confuse it with sooty mould, mealybugs or dust and spray residue.

Symptoms

  • White to grey powdery patches, mostly on the upper side of young leaves and on green twigs
  • The powder rubs off easily with a finger, leaving a pale or yellowish patch
  • Young leaves turn pale greyish-green, twist and curl
  • White powder on flowers and young fruit, and young fruit drop early
  • In bad attacks, leaves fall, young shoots wither and twigs die back

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Oidium tingitaninum (also named Acrosporium or Fibroidium tingitaninum) and related powdery mildew fungi.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores are carried by wind from infected trees
  • The fungus survives on fallen infected leaves
  • It moves on hands, clothes, tools and vehicles, and with infected nursery plants
  • Soft new flush is attacked the most
  • Shade, crowding and poor air flow in orchards and nurseries favour it

What to Do

  • Check the new flush and rub the white patches: powdery mildew rubs off as dust, mealybugs are soft insects with cottony wax, and sooty mould is black
  • Cut off badly affected shoots and collect fallen infected leaves, then burn or bury them away from the trees
  • Open up crowded canopies and thin out crowded nursery plants so air and light can get in
  • If the mildew keeps spreading on new flush, ask your agriculture officer for a recommended fungicide and the right time to spray
  • Wash hands and clean tools after working on infected trees

Don't

  • Don't move or sell plants from a nursery bed that has powdery mildew

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if white powder spreads over the new flush of many trees or nursery plants, or if young fruit drop heavily.

Prevention

  • Buy healthy plants from registered nurseries
  • Keep proper spacing and prune for air and light
  • Avoid too much nitrogen fertiliser, which gives soft, lush growth
  • Remove fallen leaves and dead twigs from under the trees
  • Clean tools and equipment between orchards

Problems That Look Similar

  • Sooty mould

    A black, soot-like coating that grows on sticky honeydew from insects; not white.

  • Citrus mealybug

    White, waxy, cottony insects that move slowly and hide in leaf veins, under the fruit cap and where fruits touch; not a thin dust.
