Loose Smut of Wheat

Wheat ears turn into black, sooty powder instead of grain; later only a bare stalk is left.

Overview

Loose smut is a seed-borne fungal disease of wheat. The fungus hides inside seed that looks completely healthy. When this seed is sown, the fungus grows inside the plant without clear signs, and at heading the ear comes out as a mass of black powder instead of grain.

In Pakistan smutted ears are seen when the rabi wheat crop comes into ear, usually in February and March depending on the area and sowing date. Every smutted ear is a lost ear. Its spores blow onto the flowers of nearby healthy plants and infect their seed, which carries the disease into next year's crop. The real cure is clean seed and seed treatment before sowing.

Symptoms

  • Before heading, infected plants look almost normal; sometimes leaves are a little darker and stiffer
  • Smutted ears often come out a few days earlier than healthy ears
  • Ears are full of olive-black, sooty powder in place of grains
  • At first the powder is covered by a thin grey skin that soon breaks
  • Wind blows the powder away within days, leaving a bare stalk with a few bits of chaff
  • Smutted plants are scattered through the field rather than in one patch

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Ustilago tritici (seed-borne fungus).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Infected seed is the main source; the fungus lives inside seed that looks normal
  • Spores from smutted ears are carried by wind to the open flowers of nearby healthy plants
  • Cool, humid weather at flowering favours new infection
  • Saving your own seed from an infected field year after year keeps the disease going and can increase it
  • Susceptible varieties are affected more

What to Do

  • Walk the field soon after heading and see roughly how many smutted ears there are
  • Pull out smutted plants early in the morning before the powder blows away; slip a bag over the ear first, then bury the plants away from the field
  • Mark this field so its grain is not kept as seed
  • Ask your agriculture officer about certified seed and an approved fungicide seed treatment for the next sowing

Don't

  • Don't save, sell or exchange seed from a field that had smutted ears
  • Don't carry smutted ears through the healthy crop

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if you see more than a few smutted ears, or before sowing if you plan to use your own saved seed, so it can be checked and treated correctly.

Prevention

  • Sow certified, disease-free seed from a trusted source
  • Treat seed before sowing with a systemic fungicide approved by your agriculture department; surface-only treatments do not reach the fungus inside the seed
  • Grow a variety recommended for your area
  • Keep a separate seed plot and remove smutted plants from it as soon as ears appear
  • Hot-water seed treatment can work, but only under expert guidance, as it can damage germination

Problems That Look Similar

  • Karnal bunt

    Only part of some grains turns black and smells like rotten fish; the ear looks normal from outside.

  • Flag smut

    Long grey-black stripes on leaves and leaf sheaths, with twisted, stunted plants, not black ears.

  • Hill bunt (stinking smut)

    Grains keep their shape but are full of black, fishy-smelling powder; the ear is not blown bare.
