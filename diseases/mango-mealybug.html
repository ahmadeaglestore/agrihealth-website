Mango Mealybug (Gadhiri)

Flat white mealy insects crawl up the trunk in winter and cluster on shoots and flowers, making them sticky.

Overview

The mango mealybug, known to many growers as gadhiri, is a large, flat, white-powdered sucking insect. Young ones (crawlers) climb the trunk and settle on tender shoots, flower clusters and young fruit, sucking sap. Flowers dry and drop, fruit set falls and honeydew leads to black sooty mould. It also attacks citrus, guava and other trees.

It has one generation a year. Females lay eggs in the soil around the tree, where they rest through summer and autumn. In winter, around December to February, the tiny crawlers hatch and climb up the trunk. Numbers on the tree build up through spring, and in late spring adult females come down to the soil to lay eggs again. Stopping the crawlers at the trunk is the key to control.

Symptoms

  • Tiny pinkish-brown crawlers moving up the trunk in winter
  • Flat, oval, white-powdered insects in groups on shoots, flower clusters, leaf undersides and bark cracks
  • Flower clusters and tender shoots wilt and dry; flowers and small fruits drop
  • Sticky honeydew on leaves, with ants running up and down the trunk
  • Black sooty mould covering leaves and fruit
  • In heavy attack, leaves yellow and fall and the tree looks weak

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Drosicha mangiferae (giant mango mealybug).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Eggs lie in the soil around the trunk and hatch in winter
  • Crawlers climb the trunk, or cross from touching branches of nearby trees
  • Weeds, debris and untilled soil under the tree protect the eggs
  • Ants protect mealybugs from their natural enemies
  • Moving soil or plants from infested orchards can carry eggs

What to Do

  • In early winter (around December), before crawlers climb, tie a smooth plastic sheet or sticky band around the trunk about a metre above the ground and keep it clean and sticky
  • Every few days, scrape crawlers collected below the band into a container and destroy them
  • Prune branches that touch the ground or neighbouring trees so crawlers cannot bypass the band
  • If many mealybugs are already on the tree, ask your agriculture officer whether a spray is needed and which product is approved

Don't

  • Do not leave weeds and debris under the tree, where eggs and crawlers shelter
  • Do not let bands become dusty or covered, or crawlers will cross over them

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if you see many crawlers on the trunks in winter or white mealybugs on the flower clusters in spring.

Prevention

  • Plough or hoe the soil under and around trees in autumn and early winter to expose eggs to sun and birds
  • Keep the orchard clean and free of weeds
  • Band all trunks every winter before crawlers start climbing
  • Protect natural enemies such as ladybird beetles that eat mealybugs
  • Remove ant nests around trunks and check nearby citrus and guava trees too

Problems That Look Similar

  • Cotton mealybug

    Smaller, waxy mealybugs on cotton and vegetables; they do not climb mango trunks in winter.

  • Powdery mildew of mango

    A flat white powder on flowers that does not move or form insects.

  • Mango hoppers

    Small, wedge-shaped insects that jump; no white wax.
