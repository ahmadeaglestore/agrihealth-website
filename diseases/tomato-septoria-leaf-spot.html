Septoria Leaf Spot of Tomato

Many small round spots with dark edges, grey centres and tiny black dots, starting on the lowest leaves.

Overview

Septoria leaf spot is a common fungal disease of tomato leaves. It makes many small, round spots, about 3 mm across, with a dark brown edge and a grey or light brown centre. Inside each spot there are tiny black dots: these are the fruiting bodies of the fungus, and they release new spores.

The disease starts on the lowest leaves, often after the first fruits have set, and climbs up the plant. Fruit is rarely spotted, but heavy leaf loss weakens the plant, lowers yield and leaves fruit open to sunscald. It is most likely in dense, humid plantings when leaves stay wet for a long time, for example in rainy or foggy spells or under overhead watering.

Symptoms

  • Small water-soaked spots on the oldest, lowest leaves
  • Spots become round, about 3 mm across, with a dark brown edge and a grey or tan centre, sometimes with a yellow ring around them
  • Tiny black dots, like grains of pepper, in the centre of the spots (easy to see with a hand lens)
  • Leaves with many spots turn yellow, then brown, dry up and fall
  • The disease moves up the plant; badly hit plants lose most leaves from the bottom upward
  • Similar but darker spots can appear on stems and leaf stalks; spots on fruit are rare

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Septoria lycopersici (fungus carried on crop remains, tomato-family weeds and seed).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores splash from spotted leaves and old crop remains onto new leaves with rain and overhead watering
  • Wind, workers' hands and clothes, tools and insects also carry spores, especially when plants are wet
  • Long periods of wet leaves (about two days or more) with mild temperatures of about 20–25 °C favour infection
  • The fungus survives on tomato crop remains and on tomato-family weeds such as datura and nightshade
  • It can also come in on infected seed
  • Dense, unstaked plants that stay damp suffer most

What to Do

  • Pick off spotted lower leaves early and carry them out of the field; do not leave them on the ground
  • Water at the base of the plants (furrow or drip), in the morning, instead of wetting the leaves
  • Stay out of the crop while leaves are wet so you do not carry spores on hands and clothes
  • Remove the lowest side shoots and stake the plants so air can move and leaves dry quickly
  • If spots keep climbing up the plants, ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide spray is needed and which product is approved

Don't

  • Don't leave spotted leaves or old plants lying in or near the field
  • Don't use overhead watering on a crop that already has spots

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if spots are climbing quickly up many plants or leaves are falling across the field, or if you cannot tell whether it is Septoria, early blight or bacterial spot.

Prevention

  • Use healthy seed and disease-free nursery plants
  • Rotate tomato with crops outside the tomato family (not potato, chilli or brinjal) for about three years
  • Plough under or destroy all tomato remains after harvest
  • Control datura, nightshade and other tomato-family weeds in and around the field
  • Give plants enough space, stake them and keep their feeding balanced
  • Grow a tolerant variety where one is recommended for your area

Problems That Look Similar

  • Early blight

    Bigger brown spots with rings inside like a target, and no tiny black dots in the centre.

  • Bacterial spot

    Small dark, greasy-looking spots without a grey centre or black dots; raised scabby spots are common on fruit.

  • Target spot

    Larger brown spots with light centres and faint rings, often with sunken pits on the fruit.
