Maize Streak Virus

Thin, broken yellow-white streaks along the veins of young maize leaves; plants stunted. Rare in Pakistan.

Overview

Maize streak virus causes thin, broken yellow to white streaks along the veins of maize leaves. It is spread only by tiny Cicadulina leafhoppers; it is not carried in seed and does not spread by touch. Plants infected when young can be badly stunted and give small cobs or none at all.

This is mainly an African disease. It is widespread across Africa and nearby islands, and only a few reports exist from Asia (such as India, Indonesia, the Philippines and Yemen). The EPPO global database does not list it in Pakistan, and it is not a common problem here. Most yellow or white striping on maize leaves in Pakistan has another cause, such as zinc or magnesium deficiency or a different virus. If you really suspect maize streak, report it to the agriculture department so experts can test the plants.

Symptoms

  • Small pale round spots at the base of the youngest leaves, about 10–14 days after infection
  • The spots join into thin, broken (dotted-line) yellow-white streaks running along the veins
  • Streaks are seen mainly on the young leaves
  • In severe cases the whole leaf looks yellow-white striped
  • Plants infected early are stunted, with small or poorly filled cobs

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Maize streak virus (a mastrevirus), spread only by Cicadulina leafhoppers.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spread only by Cicadulina leafhoppers, which pick up the virus while feeding on infected plants
  • Not spread through seed, soil or by handling plants
  • Wild grasses can carry both the virus and the leafhoppers between crops
  • Overlapping maize crops grown side by side keep the virus and leafhoppers going
  • Infection of young plants causes the biggest losses

What to Do

  • First rule out more common causes in Pakistan, such as zinc deficiency, magnesium deficiency or mosaic viruses
  • Look closely in the whorl and on the leaves for small leafhoppers that jump or fly off when disturbed
  • Take clear photos and a few affected leaves to your agriculture officer, or report it to the agriculture department for testing
  • Pull out and destroy clearly streaked, stunted young plants so they do not act as a source
  • Keep grassy weeds down in and around the field

Don't

  • Don't spray insecticides before the cause has been confirmed by an expert

When to call an expert: Report to the agriculture department if you see thin, broken yellow-white streaks with stunted plants that do not match zinc or other deficiencies, so experts can test the plants.

Prevention

  • Use seed of varieties recommended by your agriculture department
  • Control grassy weeds in and around the field
  • Avoid planting new maize right next to an older maize crop; leave a gap between early and late plantings
  • Rotate maize with non-cereal crops such as pulses
  • Check young maize regularly for unusual streaking

Problems That Look Similar

  • Zinc deficiency

    Broad pale yellow to white bands on both sides of the midrib of young leaves, often in patches of the field; no fine broken streaks along every vein.

  • Mosaic viruses of maize

    Irregular light- and dark-green mosaic patches rather than fine dotted streaks; spread by aphids.

  • Magnesium deficiency

    Continuous yellow stripes between the veins, starting on the older, lower leaves.
