Leaf-eating Beetles and Caterpillars (Chewed Leaves)

Holes, chewed edges or lace-like scraped leaves on vegetables or maize, made by beetles or caterpillars.

Overview

Many insects chew plant leaves. Beetles and caterpillars are the most common. They leave holes, ragged edges, or leaves scraped until only a thin net of veins is left. This guide helps you find which insect is causing the damage, because the right action depends on the insect. In Pakistan the main culprits are the hadda (epilachna) beetle on brinjal, potato, tomato and sometimes gourds, the red pumpkin beetle on melons and gourds, tiny flea beetles, leaf beetles on maize, and caterpillars such as armyworms, semiloopers and cabbage caterpillars.

A few holes rarely reduce yield, and strong older plants can lose some leaf without harm. Damage matters most on young seedlings, which can be eaten to the ground, and when insects build up fast in warm weather. The red pumpkin beetle does the most harm to melon and gourd seedlings at the first-leaf stage, and its grubs also feed on roots and on fruit lying on the soil. Hadda beetle adults and their spiny yellow grubs scrape the leaf surface, and the leaves turn brown, dry and drop. Many caterpillars feed at night and hide by day, so you often see the damage before the insect.

Symptoms

  • Many small round 'shot holes' in young leaves (often flea beetles)
  • Leaf surface scraped between the veins in a ladder or net pattern, later turning brown and dry (hadda beetle adults and grubs)
  • Irregular holes in the leaves and flowers of melon and gourd seedlings; young seedlings may be eaten down (red pumpkin beetle)
  • Large ragged holes and chewed leaf edges, sometimes with only the main veins left (caterpillars)
  • Small dark pellets (droppings) on the leaves or in the leaf whorl where caterpillars feed
  • Rows of holes or ragged holes in maize leaves, sometimes with wet sawdust-like droppings in the whorl

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Chewing insects such as the hadda (Epilachna) beetle Henosepilachna vigintioctopunctata, red pumpkin beetle Aulacophora foveicollis, flea beetles, maize leaf beetles (e.g. Monolepta) and leaf-eating caterpillars.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Adult beetles fly in from nearby crops, weeds and old crop remains; red pumpkin beetle adults rest in the soil and plant debris during the cool months and come out as the weather warms
  • Warm weather speeds up breeding, so numbers can rise quickly in spring and summer
  • Moths lay egg masses on the leaves; young caterpillars feed together and then spread through the field
  • Weeds and related crops (for example cucumber, tori or tinda next to pumpkin) keep beetles in the area
  • Young seedlings are hit hardest because they have only a few leaves to lose

What to Do

  • Look closely under the leaves, in the shoot tips and in the maize whorl; check again with a torch after dark, when many caterpillars and beetles feed
  • Identify the insect: dull orange beetles with many black spots and spiny yellow grubs are hadda beetles; shiny orange-red beetles on gourds are red pumpkin beetles; tiny jumping black beetles are flea beetles; soft larvae with legs are caterpillars
  • Hand-pick beetles, grubs, egg clusters and groups of young caterpillars and drop them into a bowl of soapy water; early morning is best, when beetles move slowly
  • Cover melon and gourd seedlings with fine net or mosquito netting until they have several true leaves
  • Neem-based sprays can help against young caterpillars and grubs; spray in the evening and wet the underside of the leaves
  • If damage keeps spreading on young plants, ask your agriculture officer whether an insecticide is needed and which one is approved for that crop

Don't

  • Don't spray at the first few holes; first find which insect is there and whether it is still present
  • Don't kill helpful ladybirds: the hadda beetle is dull and slightly hairy with many spots, while ladybirds that eat aphids are shiny

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if seedlings are being eaten to the ground, if many plants in every row are badly damaged, or if you find many caterpillars in the maize whorl, which may be fall armyworm.

Prevention

  • Plough the field soon after harvest to expose beetles and pupae resting in the soil
  • Remove weeds and old crop remains in and around the field
  • Do not grow related crops such as cucumber, tori or tinda right next to a pumpkin or melon field
  • Sow or transplant on time so young plants are strong before the main insect season
  • Raise seedlings under insect net and check them before transplanting
  • Use light traps to catch moths where electricity is available
  • Protect birds, spiders, predatory bugs and parasitic wasps by avoiding unneeded sprays

Problems That Look Similar

  • Armyworm (Spodoptera litura)

    Groups of tiny caterpillars scraping leaves from below near a hairy egg mass; later large dark caterpillars with black spots that eat whole leaves at night.

  • Fall armyworm

    On maize: ragged holes and wet sawdust-like droppings in the whorl, and a caterpillar with an upside-down Y on the head and four dark spots in a square near the tail.

  • Grasshoppers and locusts

    Large jumping insects that bite big pieces from leaf edges in daylight; they hop away when you walk through the crop.
