Damping-off of Seedlings (Nursery Death)

Seeds fail to come up, or young seedlings get a thin, rotten stem at the soil line, fall over and die in patches.

Overview

Damping-off is the death of seeds and young seedlings caused by germs that live in almost all soils: the water moulds Pythium and Phytophthora, and the fungi Rhizoctonia and Fusarium. It can happen before the seedlings come up (pre-emergence), when seeds rot in the soil and leave gaps in the rows, or after they come up (post-emergence), when the stem rots at the soil line and the seedling falls over. Tomato, chilli, brinjal, onion, cabbage and cauliflower nurseries are often hit, and direct-sown cotton, okra and gourds can also be affected.

Seedlings are at risk only while they are young and soft; once plants grow stronger they are mostly safe. The disease is closely linked to water: wet, poorly drained, over-watered and crowded nursery beds, and cold soil that slows germination, give the worst losses. In Pakistan, cool winter nurseries such as onion (sown up to the end of November in Punjab) and nurseries hit by heavy rain or waterlogging in the monsoon are both at risk. A whole nursery can be lost within a few days, so prevention is much better than cure.

Symptoms

  • Gaps in the nursery rows because seeds rotted before coming up
  • Young seedlings with a water-soaked, thin, brown or black pinched stem at or just above the soil
  • Seedlings fall over, wilt and die, usually in patches that spread outwards across the bed
  • Roots are brown, rotten and few, and come off easily
  • White, cottony fungal growth on fallen seedlings and on the soil surface in humid weather
  • In cabbage and cauliflower, seedlings may stay standing with a thin, dark, twisted 'wire' stem
  • Surviving seedlings are weak, stunted and slow to grow

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Soil-borne Pythium and Phytophthora (water moulds), Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium species.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • The germs live in soil and plant debris and are found in most fields; they spread with soil, water splash, flowing water, dirty trays and tools, and infected seedlings
  • Wet, waterlogged, poorly drained beds and over-watering favour Pythium and Phytophthora; Rhizoctonia can also attack in drier soil
  • Thick sowing and crowded seedlings let the disease jump from plant to plant
  • Cold soil, deep shade or weak seed slow germination and keep seedlings soft for longer
  • Using the same nursery site year after year, field soil from diseased areas, or raw, unrotted manure builds up the germs

What to Do

  • Stop watering for a while and let the top of the bed dry; then water lightly, in the morning only
  • Remove collapsed seedlings with the soil around them and destroy them away from the nursery
  • Thin out crowded seedlings and open any cover so air can move and the surface can dry
  • Open drains around the bed so no water stands in it
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a fungicide or biological product approved for nursery drenching is worth using; if most seedlings are lost, sow again on a fresh, raised, well-drained site

Don't

  • Don't keep watering a nursery bed that is already wet
  • Don't transplant seedlings with dark, thin or rotten stems into the field

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if seedlings keep dying even after you reduce watering and improve drainage, or if the same nursery site fails every year.

Prevention

  • Make raised nursery beds on well-drained land with drains around them, and change the nursery site every year
  • Solarise the nursery soil in the hot summer months before sowing: water the bed, cover it tightly with clear plastic sheet for several weeks, then sow without digging deep
  • Use only well-rotted compost or manure in the nursery, never raw manure
  • Use healthy, vigorous seed and treat it with an approved fungicide or biological seed treatment recommended by your agriculture department
  • Sow thinly in lines, not too deep, so seedlings are not crowded
  • Water lightly and in the morning; avoid waterlogging and over-watering, especially in cool or cloudy weather
  • Clean trays, pots and tools before reuse, and don't bring soil from fields where seedlings died before

Problems That Look Similar

  • Cutworms

    Seedlings are cut cleanly through at soil level, the stem is not rotten, and a grey-brown caterpillar curled like a 'C' can be dug up nearby.

  • Fusarium and Verticillium wilt

    Affects older, transplanted plants that wilt with yellow lower leaves and brown streaks inside the stem; the stem base is not rotten and pinched.
