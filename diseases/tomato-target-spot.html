Target Spot of Tomato

Brown leaf spots with light centres and faint rings, and sunken, pitted spots on tomato fruit.

Overview

Target spot is a fungal disease of tomato leaves, stems and fruit. Its spots look like early blight, and when young they look almost the same as bacterial spot, so it is easy to confuse. The same fungus attacks hundreds of other plants, including cucumber, cotton and papaya.

It needs warm, humid weather: about 28–32 °C with leaves staying wet for many hours (from about 16 hours to nearly two days). Infection often starts inside dense plants, where moisture stays longest, and is easy to miss. Spots on fruit can turn into deep pits and cracks and lead to rotting after picking.

Symptoms

  • Tiny dark brown flecks on leaves, often first inside the dense lower part of the plant
  • Spots grow into round brown patches with light brown to grey centres, faint darker rings and sometimes a yellow edge
  • Spots join together; leaves turn yellow, dry and drop early
  • Dark brown oval spots on stems and leaf stalks
  • Small brown sunken flecks on fruit that become deep pits as fruit ripens; badly hit fruit may crack in an X or star shape
  • Fruit on the inner side of the plant is often spotted first

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Corynespora cassiicola (fungus with a very wide host range).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Spores are carried by wind and rain splash
  • Warm (about 28–32 °C), humid weather with long periods of wet leaves
  • Dense, unpruned plants keep leaves wet for longer
  • The fungus survives on crop remains (up to about two years on some remains), volunteer plants and weeds
  • Many other crops and weeds can carry it, so it can come from neighbouring fields

What to Do

  • Remove badly spotted lower leaves and spotted fruit and take them out of the field
  • Prune and stake plants to open them up so leaves dry faster
  • Water at the base, not over the leaves, and in the morning
  • Ask your agriculture officer to confirm, because it is easily mistaken for early blight or bacterial spot, and to advise whether a fungicide spray is needed

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if spots spread quickly in warm humid weather, leaves are dropping or fruit is getting pits, or if you cannot tell it from early blight.

Prevention

  • Rotate tomato with crops outside the tomato family
  • Use clean, healthy nursery plants
  • Remove weeds (especially tomato-family weeds), volunteer plants and crop remains
  • Space plants well so air can move through the crop
  • Do not plant a new tomato crop right next to an old, spotted one

Problems That Look Similar

  • Early blight

    Darker brown spots with clear target rings and a yellow halo, starting on the oldest leaves; fruit spots are dark and near the stem.

  • Bacterial spot

    Spots stay small, dark and greasy; fruit spots are raised and scabby, not sunken pits.

  • Septoria leaf spot

    Many very small spots with grey centres and tiny black dots.
