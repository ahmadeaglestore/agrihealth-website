White Rust of Mustard and Canola

Raised, shiny white blisters under mustard leaves, yellow spots above, and swollen, twisted flower heads.

Overview

White rust is caused by Albugo candida, a fungus-like organism (an oomycete). It is not a true rust. It makes chalk-white, raised blisters (pustules), mostly on the underside of leaves, with yellow spots on the upper side just above them. When it gets into the flowering shoots, they swell, bend and turn into thick, twisted, green 'stag heads' that make no seed. It attacks mustard (raya), turnip-type sarson and toria, and cabbage-family vegetables such as radish, cabbage and cauliflower. Most canola (Brassica napus) varieties are more resistant than raya and turnip-type crops.

It is a rabi disease. It is favoured by long, cool, dewy or foggy nights with slightly warmer days, so it builds up in the cool, damp part of winter. The organism survives as thick-walled resting spores in soil, crop remains and old stag heads, and on volunteer mustard plants and cabbage-family weeds. Stag heads cut yield directly: in canola studies, every 1% of stag heads in a field cost about 1% of yield. It often appears together with downy mildew on the same plants.

Symptoms

  • Small yellow spots on the upper side of the leaves
  • Raised, shiny, white to creamy blisters under the leaf, just below the yellow spots; they burst and release a white powder of spores
  • Blisters join into white patches; the spots above turn brown and the leaves yellow and dry
  • White blisters can also form on stems, flower stalks and pods
  • Flower stalks swell, thicken and bend; flowers stay green, fleshy and deformed ('stag head')
  • Stag heads make no seed and later turn brown and hard

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Albugo candida (a fungus-like water mould, or oomycete; not a true rust).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Resting spores survive in the soil, in crop remains and in old stag heads from the last crop
  • A few infected volunteer mustard plants or cabbage-family weeds can start the disease in nearby fields
  • Spores from the white blisters spread to other plants by wind and splashing water
  • Long, cool, dewy or foggy nights with mild days favour infection
  • Growing mustard-family crops in the same field every year, and over-irrigation or standing water, make it worse

What to Do

  • Walk through the field and turn leaves over to look for white blisters, especially in foggy weeks
  • Pull out plants with stag heads early and remove them from the field; bury or destroy them
  • Do not over-irrigate and do not let water stand in the field
  • If white rust is spreading before or at flowering, ask your agriculture officer which fungicide is recommended and when to spray
  • After harvest, collect and destroy crop remains and stag heads

Don't

  • Don't leave stag heads and crop remains lying in the field after harvest
  • Don't sow mustard or canola in the same field year after year

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if white blisters are spreading fast in foggy weather or many stag heads appear at flowering; they can confirm the disease and advise whether a spray is needed.

Prevention

  • Sow resistant or tolerant varieties recommended for your area by the agriculture department
  • Use certified, clean seed, with seed treatment by an approved fungicide if your agriculture officer advises it
  • Sow at the recommended time for your area
  • Keep two to three years between mustard-family crops in the same field; grow wheat or other crops in between
  • Remove cabbage-family weeds and volunteer mustard plants in and around the field
  • Use balanced fertiliser, including potash, as advised by your agriculture officer

Problems That Look Similar

  • Downy mildew

    Soft, greyish-white fuzzy growth under the leaf, not raised shiny blisters; it often appears together with white rust.

  • Powdery mildew of mustard

    Flat, dusty white powder that rubs off easily, mostly on older leaves, stems and pods late in the season; no blisters and no stag heads.

  • Alternaria blight

    Brown to black round spots, often with rings, on leaves and pods; no white blisters.
