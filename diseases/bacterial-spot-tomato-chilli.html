Bacterial Spot of Tomato and Chilli

Small dark, greasy-looking spots on leaves that turn yellow, and raised, scabby spots on the fruit.

Overview

Bacterial spot is a disease of tomato, chilli and capsicum (shimla mirch) caused by four closely related Xanthomonas bacteria. Some strains attack only tomato, some only chilli, and some both. It spots leaves, stems and fruit. Spotted fruit does not usually rot, but it looks scabby and sells for much less.

It is worst in warm weather (about 24–30 °C) with high humidity and frequent rain or overhead watering, so it is most likely in rainy, humid spells such as the monsoon; cool nights hold it back. The bacteria usually arrive in a field with infected seed or nursery plants, so clean seed and a clean nursery are the most important controls. Sick plants cannot be cured.

Symptoms

  • Small, dark, water-soaked or greasy-looking spots on leaves, often first on older leaves
  • Spots turn dark brown to black and stay small (usually less than 3 mm); on tomato they often have a yellow ring
  • On tomato leaves the dead centre of a spot may fall out, leaving a small hole
  • Spots join into larger dead patches, often along the leaf edges; leaves turn yellow and drop
  • Small dark spots on stems, leaf stalks and the green cap of the fruit
  • Raised, brown, rough scab-like spots on green fruit; on chilli they are often near the stem end
  • Seedlings in a badly infected nursery can lose most of their leaves

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Xanthomonas species (X. euvesicatoria, X. vesicatoria, X. perforans, X. gardneri); seed-borne bacteria.

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Infected seed and nursery plants bring the disease to new fields; young plants can carry the bacteria before spots show
  • Splashing rain, overhead watering, hands and tools spread the bacteria from plant to plant, especially when leaves are wet
  • Warm (about 24–30 °C), humid and rainy weather makes it spread fast
  • The bacteria survive in crop remains for one to two years, and on volunteer tomato and chilli plants and nightshade-family weeds

What to Do

  • Remove and destroy nursery seedlings that have spots; never transplant them
  • In the field, pull out and destroy badly spotted plants early, and pick off spotted leaves and fruit
  • Change from overhead watering to furrow or drip, and water in the morning
  • Do not tie, prune or pick while leaves are wet; wash hands and clean tools after working in affected rows
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a copper-based spray suits your crop; it only protects healthy leaves, does not cure spots, and some bacteria are already resistant to copper

Don't

  • Don't save seed from a crop that had bacterial spot
  • Don't keep spraying again and again without advice; no spray cures spots that have already formed

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer if spots appear in the nursery, if they spread quickly after rain, or if fruit is getting scabby spots.

Prevention

  • Buy certified, healthy seed; ask your agriculture department about hot-water seed treatment
  • Raise the nursery in clean soil and check every seedling before transplanting
  • Rotate away from tomato and chilli for at least one year, and longer if you can
  • Remove volunteer tomato and chilli plants and nightshade weeds
  • Plough under or destroy crop remains after harvest
  • Grow resistant varieties where available; many chilli and capsicum varieties have resistance, fewer tomato varieties do
  • Space plants well so leaves dry quickly

Problems That Look Similar

  • Septoria leaf spot

    Spots have grey centres with tiny black dots, and fruit is rarely spotted.

  • Early blight

    Larger brown spots with target-like rings inside.

  • Target spot

    Young spots look almost the same, but they grow larger with light centres and faint rings; fruit spots are sunken pits, not raised scabs.
