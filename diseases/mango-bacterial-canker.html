Bacterial Canker (Black Spot) of Mango

Raised, black, angular spots with a yellow edge on leaves; fruit gets black spots that crack and ooze.

Overview

Bacterial canker, also called bacterial black spot, is a disease of mango caused by a bacterium. It attacks leaves, twigs, flower stalks and fruit. Leaf spots are small, black, raised and angular (with straight edges along the veins). On fruit, the spots crack open and can ooze a sticky liquid full of bacteria. Badly infected leaves and fruit fall early, and spotted fruit loses its market value.

The bacteria enter through natural pores and small wounds. Rain driven by strong wind is the main way they spread and also makes the tiny wounds they need, so the disease is worst in windy, rainy weather and in exposed orchards. In Pakistan it has been found in mango orchards of Punjab, with Multan, Muzaffargarh and Khanewal among the most affected districts in one study, and no tested variety was fully resistant. The disease slows down quickly in dry weather.

Symptoms

  • Small water-soaked specks on older leaves that turn into black, raised, angular spots, often with a yellow ring (halo) around them
  • Spots join into larger dead patches; old spots dry to light brown or ash-grey, and badly hit leaves fall
  • On fruit, small dark spots, often around the tiny pores on the skin, that grow, turn black and crack open like a star
  • Sticky gum oozing from cracked fruit spots, and streaks of spots running down the fruit where rain water flowed (tear-stain pattern)
  • Dark cracks running along young twigs and fruit stalks; these twigs break easily in wind
  • Early fruit drop in badly infected trees

Causes & Spread

Main cause: Xanthomonas citri pv. mangiferaeindicae (bacterium; older name Xanthomonas campestris pv. mangiferaeindicae).

How it spreads and what makes it worse

  • Rain splash and wind-driven rain carry the bacteria from spots on leaves, twigs and fruit to new growth
  • Strong wind causes small wounds (leaves and fruit rubbing, flying sand and twigs) through which the bacteria enter
  • Infected nursery plants, grafting wood and seed stones carry the disease to new orchards and areas
  • Pruning and harvesting tools and workers' hands can move the bacteria from tree to tree
  • Warm, humid, rainy weather favours the disease; it slows down in dry periods

What to Do

  • Cut off badly spotted twigs, leaves and fruit, and burn or bury them away from the orchard
  • Collect fallen spotted leaves and fruit from under the trees
  • Clean pruning tools between trees, and avoid working in the trees while they are wet
  • Ask your agriculture officer whether a copper-based spray is advised for your orchard and when it should be used
  • At harvest, cut the fruit stalk instead of pulling fruit off, to avoid fresh wounds

Don't

  • Do not take grafting wood or seed stones from trees with black spots
  • Do not wet the leaves with overhead water; water the root zone only

When to call an expert: Contact your agriculture officer or the Mango Research Institute if black spots appear on many leaves or on fruit, especially after storms, or before you start a new orchard from nursery plants.

Prevention

  • Buy healthy, spot-free plants from a registered nursery and check new plants before planting
  • Plant windbreak trees around the orchard to cut wind speed and wounding
  • Prune every year after harvest to open the canopy so leaves dry quickly after rain
  • Use drip or basin irrigation instead of water that wets the canopy
  • Remove spotted plant parts regularly to reduce the source of bacteria
  • Keep weeds down in and around the orchard

Problems That Look Similar

  • Mango anthracnose

    Anthracnose spots are round, flat or sunken and grow fast on ripe fruit; canker spots are raised, angular, have a yellow ring and crack open on fruit.

  • Mango leaf gall midge

    Galls are small pimple-like swellings seen on both sides of the leaf, often with a tiny exit hole, and they do not appear on fruit.

  • Bacterial apical necrosis

    Mostly blackens buds, flower stalks and leaf stalks in cold, wet weather in winter and early spring; developed fruit is usually not spotted.
