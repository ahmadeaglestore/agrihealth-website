How to Check Your Crop Leaves for Early Signs of Disease

By the AgriHealth AI Team · October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Most crop diseases are much easier to manage when they are found early. A few spotted leaves in one corner of a field is a very different problem from a whole field that is already brown. The good news is that early checks do not need special equipment, only a regular routine and a careful eye.

Walk the field in a "W" pattern

Diseases often start in patches, so only checking the edge of the field near the road can miss them. Once a week, walk through the field in a W or zig-zag pattern and stop at 8 to 10 places. At each stop, look closely at several plants, not just the first one you see.

  • Check low, wet or shaded parts of the field, where many diseases start.
  • Look at both the upper and the lower leaves.
  • Turn leaves over; some problems, such as the white growth of late blight or many insects, are found underneath.

What to look for on the leaves

Disease symptoms usually follow a pattern. Notice the colour, the shape of the spots and where on the plant they appear.

  • Powder that rubs off: yellow, orange or brown powder is typical of rust diseases. If it is in straight lines on wheat, think of yellow (stripe) rust.
  • White, cotton-like growth: often powdery mildew.
  • Brown spots with rings like a target: a classic sign of early blight on tomato and potato.
  • Large dark, wet-looking patches that spread fast: a warning sign of late blight, which needs action the same day.
  • Leaves drying from the tip and edges: on rice, this can be bacterial leaf blight.
  • Curled leaves with thick, dark veins: on cotton, think of cotton leaf curl disease.

Is it a disease or something else?

Not every yellow leaf is a disease. Nutrient shortages, too much or too little water, cold nights, herbicide drift and insects can all cause damage that looks similar. A few clues help:

  • Disease usually starts in patches and spreads over days, and often shows spots, powder or growth on the leaf.
  • Nutrient or water problems are often more even across the field and follow soil type, low areas or irrigation lines.
  • Herbicide damage often follows the direction of spraying or the edge of a neighbouring field.
  • Insects can usually be seen if you look closely, especially under the leaves.

Keep simple records

Write down the date, the field, the crop stage and what you saw. Take a clear photo of the worst leaf. This helps you see whether a problem is getting worse, and it is very useful when you ask an agriculture officer for advice.

When to ask for help

Contact your local agriculture office when a problem is spreading quickly, when the upper leaves of a cereal crop are being attacked before the grain has filled, or when you are not sure what you are seeing.
